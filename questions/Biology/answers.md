## VCE Units 1 & 2

### Unit 1 AOS 1: How do cells function?

#### Cell theory and cell size

**A1.** B. All cells arise from pre-existing cells.
<!-- id: bio12a1-ct-1 -->

Explanation: Cell theory states that all living things are made of one or more cells, the cell is the basic unit of life, and all cells come from pre-existing cells. Prokaryotes lack a nucleus, and spontaneous generation was disproved.

**A2.** C. 3:1
<!-- id: bio12a1-ct-2 -->

Explanation: SA = 6 × 2² = 24 µm²; V = 2³ = 8 µm³; SA:V = 24:8 = 3:1. A 6:1 ratio would be a 1 µm cube.

**A3.** D. Its SA:V ratio decreases, so exchange per unit volume becomes less efficient.
<!-- id: bio12a1-ct-3 -->

Explanation: Volume scales with the cube of length and surface area with the square, so SA:V falls as a cell grows. Less membrane is available per unit of cytoplasm to supply materials and remove wastes.

**A4.** C. The 1 cm cube would show the greatest percentage of its volume changed colour.
<!-- id: bio12a1-ct-4 -->

Explanation: Acid diffuses inward at about the same rate (same penetration depth) in all cubes, but the smallest cube has the highest SA:V, so the largest fraction of its volume changes colour.

**A5.** B. Microvilli or a flattened, elongated shape
<!-- id: bio12a1-ct-5 -->

Explanation: Folding the membrane into microvilli or flattening the cell increases surface area without much increase in volume, raising SA:V. A sphere has the lowest SA:V of any shape for a given volume.

**A6.** Cells exchange materials (nutrients, O₂, wastes) across their plasma membrane; small cells have a high SA:V ratio so the membrane area can supply the volume quickly enough / diffusion distances to the centre stay short; large cells would have too little membrane relative to volume and could not meet metabolic demands.
<!-- id: bio12a1-ct-6 -->

Explanation:

**A7.** X: SA = 6 × 16 = 96 µm², V = 64 µm³, SA:V = 1.5:1; Y: SA = 6 × 64 = 384 µm², V = 512 µm³, SA:V = 0.75:1; cell X is more efficient because it has the higher SA:V ratio (twice that of Y).
<!-- id: bio12a1-ct-7 -->

Explanation:

**A8.** Microscopes allowed cells to be observed for the first time (e.g. Hooke's cork cells, van Leeuwenhoek's microorganisms); repeated observation of cells in all plant and animal tissues, and of cells dividing, provided the evidence that all organisms are made of cells and that cells come from pre-existing cells.
<!-- id: bio12a1-ct-8 -->

#### Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

**A9.** A. Ribosomes
<!-- id: bio12a1-pe-1 -->

Explanation: All cells have ribosomes (prokaryotes have smaller 70S ribosomes, eukaryotes 80S in the cytosol). The other structures are membrane-bound organelles found only in eukaryotes.

**A10.** D. a prokaryote.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pe-2 -->

Explanation: Circular DNA in a nucleoid region, plasmids and the absence of membrane-bound organelles are defining features of prokaryotes. Fungi and plants have cell walls but are eukaryotes with nuclei.

**A11.** B. Presence of a cell wall and large central vacuole
<!-- id: bio12a1-pe-3 -->

Explanation: Plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, a large central vacuole and often chloroplasts. Both cell types have a nucleus, plasma membrane and cytoplasm.

**A12.** B. prokaryotes 0.1–5 µm; eukaryotes 10–100 µm.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pe-4 -->

Explanation: Most prokaryotes are about 0.1–5 µm, while most eukaryotic cells are about 10–100 µm. Nanometre values are far too small for whole cells.

**A13.** D. It is a single circular chromosome located in the nucleoid region, often with plasmids.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pe-5 -->

Explanation: Prokaryotes have one main circular chromosome in the nucleoid (not membrane-bound) and may carry small circular plasmids. Linear, histone-bound DNA in a nucleus is eukaryotic.

**A14.** Any two: eukaryotes have a membrane-bound nucleus while prokaryotes have a nucleoid region; eukaryotes have membrane-bound organelles (e.g. mitochondria, ER) while prokaryotes do not; prokaryotic DNA is circular while eukaryotic DNA is linear; prokaryotes have smaller (70S) ribosomes; prokaryotes are generally much smaller.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pe-6 -->

Explanation:

**A15.** A plant (eukaryotic, photosynthetic) cell; the nucleus and mitochondria show it is eukaryotic, and the chloroplasts and cell wall together indicate a plant cell (animal cells lack both; fungi lack chloroplasts).
<!-- id: bio12a1-pe-7 -->

Explanation:

**A16.** Organelles separate incompatible reactions and keep enzymes and substrates concentrated in specific compartments (e.g. hydrolytic enzymes in lysosomes); this increases the efficiency of metabolism and allows eukaryotic cells to be larger and more complex than prokaryotes.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pe-8 -->

Explanation:

#### Organelles and their functions

**A17.** B. Mitochondrion
<!-- id: bio12a1-or-1 -->

Explanation: The mitochondrion carries out the Krebs cycle and electron transport chain, which produce most ATP. Chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis.

**A18.** D. rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus.
<!-- id: bio12a1-or-2 -->

Explanation: Secreted proteins are made on ribosomes of the rough ER and modified and packaged by the Golgi apparatus as part of the secretory pathway. Smooth ER synthesises lipids.

**A19.** C. ribosome on rough ER → transport vesicle → Golgi apparatus → secretory vesicle → plasma membrane
<!-- id: bio12a1-or-3 -->

Explanation: Proteins are synthesised by ribosomes on the rough ER, carried in vesicles to the Golgi for modification and packaging, then secretory vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane (exocytosis).

**A20.** D. be damaged by released hydrolytic enzymes digesting its own components.
<!-- id: bio12a1-or-4 -->

Explanation: Lysosomes contain hydrolytic enzymes. If released into the cytosol they can break down cell components. ATP production and protein synthesis depend on other organelles.

**A21.** A. Smooth ER: synthesis of lipids and detoxification
<!-- id: bio12a1-or-5 -->

Explanation: Smooth ER makes lipids (including steroids) and detoxifies drugs, e.g. in liver cells. Ribosomes synthesise polypeptides, the Golgi packages them, and the nucleolus makes ribosomal subunits.

**A22.** Double membrane-bound organelle; contains stacks of thylakoid membranes (grana) containing chlorophyll, surrounded by fluid stroma; site of photosynthesis, converting light energy into chemical energy in glucose (light-dependent reactions on thylakoids, light-independent reactions in stroma).
<!-- id: bio12a1-or-6 -->

Explanation:

**A23.** Flight muscle contracts repeatedly and has a very high energy demand; mitochondria carry out aerobic respiration producing ATP, so more mitochondria supply the large amounts of ATP needed for contraction, whereas skin cells require far less.
<!-- id: bio12a1-or-7 -->

Explanation:

**A24.** The nucleus contains the DNA (chromosomes) holding the genetic instructions; genes are transcribed into mRNA which leaves through nuclear pores to ribosomes, so the nucleus controls which proteins (including enzymes) are made and hence cell activities.
<!-- id: bio12a1-or-8 -->

Explanation:

#### Plasma membrane structure

**A25.** B. the scattered pattern of proteins embedded in the phospholipid bilayer.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pm-1 -->

Explanation: 'Mosaic' describes the varied proteins dotted through the bilayer; 'fluid' describes the lateral movement of phospholipids and proteins.

**A26.** D. their phosphate heads are hydrophilic and their fatty acid tails are hydrophobic.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pm-2 -->

Explanation: The amphipathic phospholipids orient with hydrophilic heads facing the aqueous fluid on both sides and hydrophobic tails facing inward, away from water.

**A27.** A. Cholesterol
<!-- id: bio12a1-pm-3 -->

Explanation: Cholesterol reduces fluidity at high temperatures and prevents tight packing of phospholipids at low temperatures. Glycoproteins are involved in cell recognition.

**A28.** B. glycoprotein.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pm-4 -->

Explanation: Glycoproteins and glycolipids on the outer surface act as recognition markers (e.g. MHC markers). Phospholipids and cholesterol are structural.

**A29.** B. have kinks that prevent tight packing, keeping the membrane fluid at low temperatures.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pm-5 -->

Explanation: Double bonds create kinks in unsaturated tails, so phospholipids cannot pack closely, which maintains fluidity in cold conditions.

**A30.** A phospholipid bilayer with hydrophilic heads facing outward and hydrophobic tails inward; proteins (integral/transmembrane and peripheral) are embedded in or attached to the bilayer in a mosaic pattern, with cholesterol and glycoproteins/glycolipids present; components can move laterally, so the membrane is fluid.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pm-6 -->

Explanation:

**A31.** O₂ is small and non-polar so it dissolves in and passes through the hydrophobic core of the bilayer; Na⁺ is charged and is repelled by the hydrophobic tails, so it needs channel or carrier proteins to cross.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pm-7 -->

Explanation:

**A32.** Any two: transport (channel or carrier proteins); receptors for signalling molecules such as hormones; enzymes; cell recognition (glycoproteins); attachment/adhesion to other cells or the cytoskeleton.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pm-8 -->

Explanation:

#### Passive transport across membranes

**A33.** D. requires membrane channel or carrier proteins.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pt-1 -->

Explanation: Facilitated diffusion is passive (no ATP) and occurs down a concentration gradient but needs a protein channel or carrier. Moving against a gradient is active transport.

**A34.** A. Water leaves the cell and it shrinks (crenates).
<!-- id: bio12a1-pt-2 -->

Explanation: In a hypertonic solution the solute concentration outside is higher, so water moves out by osmosis and the cell shrinks. Osmosis is the movement of water, not solute.

**A35.** B. the cell wall resists expansion, so the cell becomes turgid.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pt-3 -->

Explanation: Water enters by osmosis, but the rigid cellulose wall exerts pressure back, so the cell becomes turgid rather than bursting.

**A36.** A. The potato cells are isotonic to about 0.3 mol L⁻¹ sucrose.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pt-4 -->

Explanation: No net mass change means no net water movement, so the cell sap is approximately isotonic to 0.3 mol L⁻¹. At 0.6 mol L⁻¹ the solution is hypertonic to the cells, so they lost water.

**A37.** A. molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pt-5 -->

Explanation: Higher temperature increases the kinetic energy of particles, so they move and collide more, increasing diffusion rate. Diffusion does not use ATP.

**A38.** The net movement of water molecules across a selectively (partially) permeable membrane; from a region of lower solute concentration (higher free water concentration) to a region of higher solute concentration.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pt-6 -->

Explanation:

**A39.** Cl⁻ ions normally diffuse out through CFTR channels into the mucus (facilitated diffusion); without this, solute concentration outside the cell stays low; so less water follows by osmosis into the mucus, leaving it dehydrated, thick and sticky.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pt-7 -->

Explanation:

**A40.** Glucose is a relatively large, polar molecule that cannot pass through the hydrophobic core of the phospholipid bilayer; it needs specific carrier (GLUT) proteins to move down its concentration gradient.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pt-8 -->

Explanation:

#### Active transport, endocytosis and exocytosis

**A41.** D. 3 Na⁺ out of the cell and 2 K⁺ in, using ATP.
<!-- id: bio12a1-at-1 -->

Explanation: The Na⁺/K⁺ pump uses one ATP per cycle to move 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, both against their concentration gradients.

**A42.** C. phagocytosis.
<!-- id: bio12a1-at-2 -->

Explanation: Phagocytosis ('cell eating') is a type of endocytosis in which large solid particles are engulfed in a vesicle. Pinocytosis takes in fluid.

**A43.** D. requires ATP from respiration and so is active transport.
<!-- id: bio12a1-at-3 -->

Explanation: Uptake against a concentration gradient that stops when respiration (ATP production) is blocked indicates active transport. Passive processes would not depend on ATP.

**A44.** B. Exocytosis
<!-- id: bio12a1-at-4 -->

Explanation: Insulin is a protein packaged into secretory vesicles by the Golgi; the vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents by exocytosis.

**A45.** B. all carrier proteins being in use (saturated).
<!-- id: bio12a1-at-5 -->

Explanation: Carrier-mediated transport plateaus when all carrier proteins are occupied. Dependence on oxygen indicates ATP is needed, so it is active transport.

**A46.** Similarity: both use specific membrane proteins; difference: active transport moves substances against their concentration gradient whereas facilitated diffusion moves them down the gradient; active transport requires energy from ATP whereas facilitated diffusion is passive.
<!-- id: bio12a1-at-6 -->

Explanation:

**A47.** The plasma membrane folds inward (invaginates) around the material outside the cell; the membrane pinches off to form a vesicle that carries the material into the cytoplasm; it requires energy (ATP) and is a form of bulk transport.
<!-- id: bio12a1-at-7 -->

Explanation:

**A48.** Active transport requires ATP to power protein pumps; mitochondria produce most ATP through aerobic respiration, so many mitochondria supply the energy needed for continuous active transport.
<!-- id: bio12a1-at-8 -->

### Unit 1 AOS 2: Cell division and systems

#### Binary fission

**A49.** C. prokaryotes such as bacteria.
<!-- id: bio12a1-bf-1 -->

Explanation: Binary fission is how prokaryotes reproduce asexually. Eukaryotic cells divide by mitosis and cytokinesis.

**A50.** A. DNA replicates → copies move to opposite ends as the cell elongates → septum/cell wall forms → two cells separate
<!-- id: bio12a1-bf-2 -->

Explanation: The circular chromosome is copied, the copies separate as the cell elongates, then the membrane pinches in and a new wall (septum) forms between them. There is no spindle or nuclear envelope in prokaryotes.

**A51.** C. 64
<!-- id: bio12a1-bf-3 -->

Explanation: 2 hours = 120 min = 6 generations. 2⁶ = 64 cells.

**A52.** D. Because each receives a copy of the replicated circular chromosome.
<!-- id: bio12a1-bf-4 -->

Explanation: The single chromosome is replicated and one copy goes to each daughter cell, so they are clones. Crossing over and meiosis occur in sexual reproduction of eukaryotes.

**A53.** C. Formation of a mitotic spindle
<!-- id: bio12a1-bf-5 -->

Explanation: Prokaryotes lack spindle fibres made from microtubules. Both processes involve DNA replication, cytoplasm division and produce identical cells.

**A54.** The circular chromosome (and any plasmids) is replicated; the cell elongates and the two chromosome copies move to opposite ends; the plasma membrane pinches inward and a new cell wall (septum) forms, splitting the cell into two genetically identical daughter cells.
<!-- id: bio12a1-bf-6 -->

Explanation:

**A55.** 4 h = 8 generations; 10 × 2⁸ = 10 × 256 = 2560 cells; growth slows because nutrients run out / wastes accumulate / space becomes limited.
<!-- id: bio12a1-bf-7 -->

Explanation:

**A56.** Binary fission is a simple, rapid process with short generation times (e.g. ~20 min); the number of cells doubles each generation, so the population grows exponentially and can reach very large numbers quickly.
<!-- id: bio12a1-bf-8 -->

Explanation:

#### The eukaryotic cell cycle and mitosis

**A57.** D. S
<!-- id: bio12a1-cc-1 -->

Explanation: DNA synthesis occurs in the S phase of interphase. G1 and G2 are growth phases, and M is mitosis.

**A58.** C. Anaphase
<!-- id: bio12a1-cc-2 -->

Explanation: In anaphase the centromeres split and spindle fibres pull sister chromatids to opposite poles. In metaphase chromosomes align at the equator.

**A59.** B. 46
<!-- id: bio12a1-cc-3 -->

Explanation: Mitosis produces two daughter cells genetically identical to the parent, each with the full diploid number of 46.

**A60.** B. 1 hour
<!-- id: bio12a1-cc-4 -->

Explanation: Time is proportional to the fraction of cells in that stage: 10/200 × 20 h = 1 hour.

**A61.** A. a cell plate forms between the two nuclei.
<!-- id: bio12a1-cc-5 -->

Explanation: Plant cells have rigid walls, so vesicles from the Golgi form a cell plate that becomes the new wall. Animal cells pinch in by a cleavage furrow.

**A62.** Prophase: chromosomes condense and become visible as two sister chromatids joined at a centromere; the nuclear envelope breaks down and spindle fibres begin to form from the centrosomes; metaphase: chromosomes line up along the equator (metaphase plate) with spindle fibres attached to each centromere.
<!-- id: bio12a1-cc-6 -->

Explanation:

**A63.** Checkpoints are control points where the cell checks conditions before proceeding; G1 checks cell size, nutrients and DNA damage before replication, and G2 checks that DNA has been replicated completely and correctly before mitosis; if problems are detected the cycle is halted for repair or apoptosis.
<!-- id: bio12a1-cc-7 -->

Explanation:

**A64.** Mitosis produces genetically identical daughter cells; this allows growth, repair/replacement of damaged or worn-out cells, and asexual reproduction, while keeping the chromosome number constant.
<!-- id: bio12a1-cc-8 -->

Explanation:

#### Apoptosis and cancer

**A65.** B. programmed cell death that occurs in a controlled sequence.
<!-- id: bio12a1-ap-1 -->

Explanation: Apoptosis is programmed, controlled cell death. Uncontrolled death from injury is necrosis; spreading cancer is metastasis.

**A66.** D. Formation of membrane-bound apoptotic bodies that are engulfed by phagocytes
<!-- id: bio12a1-ap-2 -->

Explanation: In apoptosis the cell shrinks, its DNA fragments and the membrane blebs into apoptotic bodies that phagocytes engulf without inflammation. Swelling and bursting describe necrosis.

**A67.** A. break down cell components during apoptosis.
<!-- id: bio12a1-ap-3 -->

Explanation: Caspases are proteases activated in apoptosis that break down proteins and the cytoskeleton, leading to dismantling of the cell.

**A68.** A. cells with damaged DNA are no longer halted at checkpoints or sent into apoptosis.
<!-- id: bio12a1-ap-4 -->

Explanation: p53 halts the cell cycle when DNA is damaged and can trigger apoptosis. Without it, damaged cells keep dividing and accumulate mutations.

**A69.** C. apoptosis.
<!-- id: bio12a1-ap-5 -->

Explanation: Webbing between digits is removed by apoptosis during embryonic development, which shapes structures without causing inflammation.

**A70.** The intrinsic pathway is triggered by signals from within the cell (e.g. DNA damage) causing mitochondria to release cytochrome c, which activates caspases; the extrinsic pathway is triggered by external signal molecules (e.g. from immune cells) binding to death receptors on the plasma membrane, which activates caspases.
<!-- id: bio12a1-ap-6 -->

Explanation:

**A71.** Proto-oncogenes code for proteins that promote normal cell division; a mutation can convert it into an oncogene that is permanently 'switched on' / overactive; cells divide uncontrollably regardless of signals, forming a mass of cells (tumour).
<!-- id: bio12a1-ap-7 -->

Explanation:

**A72.** Mitosis replaces cells while apoptosis removes old, damaged or unneeded cells; if apoptosis is too low, cells accumulate (e.g. cancer); if too high, tissue is lost (e.g. degenerative disease), so balance maintains a constant, healthy cell number.
<!-- id: bio12a1-ap-8 -->

Explanation:

#### Stem cells and specialisation

**A73.** C. totipotent.
<!-- id: bio12a1-sc-1 -->

Explanation: Totipotent cells (e.g. the zygote) can form all cell types including extra-embryonic tissue. Pluripotent cells (embryonic stem cells) form all body cell types but not the placenta.

**A74.** D. multipotent.
<!-- id: bio12a1-sc-2 -->

Explanation: Multipotent stem cells form a limited range of cell types within a particular tissue or lineage.

**A75.** A. express different genes.
<!-- id: bio12a1-sc-3 -->

Explanation: All somatic cells contain the same DNA, but differentiation involves switching specific genes on or off, producing different proteins.

**A76.** A. the inner cell mass of a blastocyst.
<!-- id: bio12a1-sc-4 -->

Explanation: Embryonic stem cells come from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst (about 5 days after fertilisation) and are pluripotent.

**A77.** D. It can self-renew by mitosis and can differentiate into specialised cells.
<!-- id: bio12a1-sc-5 -->

Explanation: Stem cells are unspecialised cells that can divide repeatedly (self-renew) and differentiate into specialised cell types. Adult stem cells exist in many tissues.

**A78.** Advantage: they are pluripotent, so can potentially form any body cell type to treat many diseases (e.g. replace damaged nerve or heart cells); concern: obtaining them destroys an embryo, which some people believe has the moral status of a person.
<!-- id: bio12a1-sc-6 -->

Explanation:

**A79.** Function is to transport oxygen; it lacks a nucleus and most organelles so there is more room for haemoglobin, and its biconcave disc shape gives a high SA:V for rapid diffusion of O₂ (and flexibility to pass through capillaries).
<!-- id: bio12a1-sc-7 -->

Explanation:

**A80.** Specialised cells of a similar type form tissues (e.g. cardiac muscle cells form cardiac muscle tissue); different tissues work together to form an organ (e.g. the heart contains muscle, nervous and connective tissue); organs work together in an organ system (e.g. heart and blood vessels form the circulatory system).
<!-- id: bio12a1-sc-8 -->

Explanation:

#### Plant transport systems

**A81.** A. water and dissolved minerals from roots to leaves.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pl-1 -->

Explanation: Xylem carries water and mineral ions upward from roots. Phloem carries sugars (mainly sucrose) in both directions.

**A82.** D. Transpiration pull, relying on cohesion and adhesion of water molecules
<!-- id: bio12a1-pl-2 -->

Explanation: Evaporation from leaves through stomata creates tension that pulls a continuous column of water up, held together by cohesion (hydrogen bonds) and adhesion to xylem walls. Xylem vessels are dead and cannot actively transport.

**A83.** B. translocation.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pl-3 -->

Explanation: Translocation is the movement of sugars and other organic compounds in phloem from sources (e.g. leaves) to sinks (e.g. roots, fruits).

**A84.** D. removes water vapour near the leaf, increasing the concentration gradient for evaporation.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pl-4 -->

Explanation: Wind removes the humid layer around the leaf, steepening the water vapour gradient between the air spaces and the atmosphere, so transpiration and water uptake increase.

**A85.** B. They are living cells with sieve plates, supported by companion cells.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pl-5 -->

Explanation: Sieve tube elements are living but lack a nucleus; companion cells supply their metabolic needs. Lignified dead cells describe xylem vessels.

**A86.** Water enters root hair cells by osmosis because the cell sap has a lower water potential / higher solute concentration than the soil (minerals are actively transported in); water moves across the cortex via the cell walls and cytoplasm; at the endodermis the Casparian strip forces water through the cytoplasm before it enters the xylem.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pl-6 -->

Explanation:

**A87.** When water loss exceeds uptake, the plant closes its stomata (guard cells lose turgor) to conserve water; less water vapour can diffuse out of the leaf, so transpiration rate decreases.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pl-7 -->

Explanation:

**A88.** Xylem consists of dead, hollow, lignified vessels while phloem consists of living sieve tube elements and companion cells; xylem transports water and minerals while phloem transports sugars (sucrose) and other organic compounds; xylem flow is upward only (from roots) while phloem transports sugars in both directions from sources to sinks.
<!-- id: bio12a1-pl-8 -->

#### Digestive system

**A89.** B. small intestine.
<!-- id: bio12a2-dig-1 -->

Explanation: The small intestine receives pancreatic enzymes and bile, and its villi absorb the products of digestion. The stomach begins protein digestion but absorbs very little, apart from substances such as alcohol.

**A90.** D. increase the surface area available for absorption.
<!-- id: bio12a2-dig-2 -->

Explanation: Villi and microvilli greatly increase the surface area of the intestinal lining, which increases the rate of absorption. Peristalsis is done by the smooth muscle layers, not the villi.

**A91.** A. Bile is produced by the liver, stored in the gallbladder and emulsifies lipids, increasing their surface area for lipase.
<!-- id: bio12a2-dig-3 -->

Explanation: The liver makes bile and the gallbladder only stores and concentrates it, so bile still flows after removal. Bile contains no enzymes; it emulsifies fats mechanically so lipase can act on a larger surface area.

**A92.** C. pepsin, secreted in the stomach.
<!-- id: bio12a2-dig-4 -->

Explanation: The stomach is highly acidic (about pH 1.5–3), and pepsin works best in these conditions. Amylase, lipase and maltase work in roughly neutral to slightly alkaline conditions.

**A93.** A. Absorbing water and some minerals and vitamins, and forming faeces
<!-- id: bio12a2-dig-5 -->

Explanation: Undigested material entering the large intestine is mostly water and fibre. The colon reabsorbs water and electrolytes, gut bacteria produce some vitamins (such as vitamin K), and faeces are formed. Amino acid absorption happens in the small intestine.

**A94.** Mechanical digestion physically breaks food into smaller pieces without changing its chemical make-up, e.g. chewing by teeth or churning in the stomach; chemical digestion uses enzymes to break the bonds in large molecules to form smaller molecules, e.g. amylase hydrolysing starch to maltose or pepsin breaking proteins into polypeptides.
<!-- id: bio12a2-dig-6 -->

Explanation:

**A95.** It is very long and folded, with villi and microvilli, giving a very large surface area for absorption; the epithelium of each villus is only one cell thick, so the diffusion distance into the blood is short; each villus has a dense capillary network (and a lacteal for lipids) that carries absorbed nutrients away, keeping a steep concentration gradient.
<!-- id: bio12a2-dig-7 -->

Explanation:

**A96.** Flattened villi greatly reduce the surface area of the small intestine; so the rate of absorption of nutrients such as glucose, amino acids, iron and vitamins decreases; the person may show malnutrition, weight loss, fatigue or anaemia even though they eat enough (undigested or unabsorbed material may also cause diarrhoea).
<!-- id: bio12a2-dig-8 -->

Explanation:

#### Endocrine and excretory systems

**A97.** B. being carried in the bloodstream and binding to specific receptors on or in target cells.
<!-- id: bio12a2-endex-1 -->

Explanation: Endocrine glands are ductless and secrete hormones into the blood. Only cells with the matching receptor respond. Ducts are a feature of exocrine glands.

**A98.** C. nephron.
<!-- id: bio12a2-endex-2 -->

Explanation: Each kidney contains about a million nephrons, each of which filters blood and modifies the filtrate into urine. The ureter only carries urine to the bladder.

**A99.** D. Plasma proteins
<!-- id: bio12a2-endex-3 -->

Explanation: Filtration at the glomerulus is based on size. Water, glucose, urea and ions pass into the capsule, but large plasma proteins (and blood cells) are too large to pass through the filtration barrier.

**A100.** A. completely reabsorbed into the blood, mainly in the proximal convoluted tubule.
<!-- id: bio12a2-endex-4 -->

Explanation: All filtered glucose is normally reabsorbed by active transport in the proximal convoluted tubule. Glucose appears in urine only when blood glucose is so high that the carriers are saturated, as in untreated diabetes mellitus.

**A101.** B. secretes hormones into the blood and digestive enzymes through a duct.
<!-- id: bio12a2-endex-5 -->

Explanation: The islets of Langerhans secrete insulin and glucagon into the blood (endocrine), while other pancreatic cells release digestive enzymes into the duodenum through the pancreatic duct (exocrine). Bile and glycogen storage are liver functions.

**A102.** Filtration: blood pressure forces water and small solutes (glucose, salts, urea) out of the glomerulus into the Bowman's capsule; reabsorption: useful substances such as glucose, amino acids, many ions and much of the water move from the tubule back into the blood; secretion: extra wastes or ions such as H⁺, K⁺ and some drugs are actively moved from the blood into the tubule.
<!-- id: bio12a2-endex-6 -->

Explanation:

**A103.** A hormone can only act on cells that have receptors complementary in shape to that hormone; only these target cells bind the hormone and start a response, while other cells lack the receptor and are not affected.
<!-- id: bio12a2-endex-7 -->

Explanation:

**A104.** The anterior pituitary secretes tropic hormones such as TSH, ACTH, FSH and LH; these stimulate other endocrine glands (thyroid, adrenal cortex, gonads) to release their own hormones, so it controls many glands; however, the pituitary is itself controlled by releasing hormones from the hypothalamus (e.g. TRH), which links the nervous system to the endocrine system.
<!-- id: bio12a2-endex-8 -->

### Unit 1 AOS 3: Homeostasis

#### Stimulus-response model and negative feedback

**A105.** C. Stimulus → receptor → modulator → effector → response
<!-- id: bio12a2-srm-1 -->

Explanation: A change (stimulus) is detected by a receptor, which sends a message to a modulator (control centre). The modulator signals an effector, which produces the response.

**A106.** B. the response counteracts the original change, returning the variable towards its set point.
<!-- id: bio12a2-srm-2 -->

Explanation: In negative feedback the response reverses the change that triggered it, keeping the variable within a narrow range. A response that amplifies the change is positive feedback.

**A107.** A. modulator.
<!-- id: bio12a2-srm-3 -->

Explanation: The medulla receives information from the receptors and coordinates a response, so it is the modulator (control centre). The heart is the effector and the rise in blood pressure is the stimulus.

**A108.** D. Release of oxytocin during childbirth, which causes stronger contractions that trigger more oxytocin release
<!-- id: bio12a2-srm-4 -->

**A161.** A. prophase I, between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.

#### Pedigree charts and modes of inheritance

**A193.** B. Autosomal recessive

Explanation: Unaffected parents having an affected child means the trait is recessive (both parents are carriers). **A194.** C. An affected female has an unaffected son

Explanation: An X-linked recessive female (XᵃXᵃ) passes an Xᵃ to every son, and sons have only one X, so all her sons would be affected. An unaffected son of an affected mother rules it out. An affected father never passes his X to his sons, so an unaffected son is expected.

**A195.** C. 50%

**A199.** Autosomal dominant traits usually appear in every generation and every affected person has at least one affected parent, whereas recessive traits can skip generations; two affected parents can have an unaffected child with a dominant trait, but two affected parents with a recessive trait (both homozygous recessive) will only have affected children.

### Unit 2 AOS 2: Reproduction and adaptation

#### Asexual reproduction

**A201.** B. Strawberry plants producing new plants from runners
<!-- id: bio12a3-asex-1 -->

Explanation: Runners (stolons) grow out from the parent strawberry plant and form new, genetically identical plants by mitosis. That is vegetative propagation. Seed production after pollination is sexual reproduction.

**A202.** B. budding
<!-- id: bio12a3-asex-2 -->

Explanation: Budding produces a smaller outgrowth that gets a copy of the nucleus by mitosis and then separates. In binary fission (e.g. bacteria) a cell divides into two cells of roughly equal size. Fragmentation means a body breaks into pieces that each regrow.

**A203.** B. An unfertilised egg develops into a new individual
<!-- id: bio12a3-asex-3 -->

Explanation: Parthenogenesis is when an unfertilised egg develops into an embryo. It happens in some insects, reptiles, fish and others. No sperm is involved, so stored sperm is not the explanation, and there is no father.

**A204.** B. A single new disease could infect and kill the whole crop because there is little genetic variation
<!-- id: bio12a3-asex-4 -->

Explanation: Asexually propagated plants are clones. If one is susceptible to a pathogen, they all are, so the whole population is vulnerable (as seen with Panama disease in bananas). Asexual propagation usually gets plants to maturity faster, not slower.

**A205.** B. Asexual reproduction allows rapid population growth in a stable environment because every individual can produce offspring
<!-- id: bio12a3-asex-5 -->

Explanation: Asexual reproduction needs only one parent, there is no need to find a mate, and every individual can reproduce. Populations can therefore grow quickly, which suits stable conditions. It uses mitosis (or binary fission), not meiosis and fertilisation, and gives little genetic diversity.

**A206.** The circular chromosome is replicated and the two copies attach to the plasma membrane; the cell elongates, separating the copies, and the membrane and cell wall pinch in across the middle to form two daughter cells; each daughter receives an identical copy of the replicated DNA, so they are genetically identical unless a mutation occurred during replication.
<!-- id: bio12a3-asex-6 -->

Explanation:

**A207.** Advantages: no mate is needed so isolated individuals can reproduce; reproduction is fast and energy-efficient, allowing rapid population growth; (also accept: successful genotypes are passed on unchanged). Disadvantages: offspring have little genetic variation, so the population may not survive a change in the environment or a new disease; harmful mutations can build up in the lineage; offspring may compete closely with the parent for resources.
<!-- id: bio12a3-asex-7 -->

Explanation:

**A208.** In spring and summer food is plentiful and conditions are stable, so asexual reproduction allows rapid population growth to exploit resources; in autumn conditions change and become harsher, so sexual reproduction produces genetically diverse offspring (and overwintering eggs), increasing the chance some will survive the changed conditions.
<!-- id: bio12a3-asex-8 -->

#### Sexual reproduction and genetic diversity

**A209.** A. Prophase I
<!-- id: bio12a3-sex-1 -->

Explanation: Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) in prophase I, and crossing over swaps segments between non-sister chromatids at chiasmata. Metaphase I is when independent assortment happens, as homologous pairs line up in random orientation.

**A210.** C. 16
<!-- id: bio12a3-sex-2 -->

Explanation: The number of combinations is 2ⁿ, where n is the haploid number. Here n = 4, so 2⁴ = 16. Using 2n = 8 as the exponent would give 2⁸ = 256, which is wrong.

**A211.** B. Anaphase I of meiosis
<!-- id: bio12a3-sex-3 -->

Explanation: In anaphase I the homologous chromosomes are pulled to opposite poles, which halves the chromosome number. In anaphase II and in mitotic anaphase, it is the sister chromatids that separate.

**A212.** B. 47 chromosomes (a trisomy)
<!-- id: bio12a3-sex-4 -->

Explanation: A normal human gamete has 23 chromosomes. A gamete with 24 fused with a normal gamete gives 24 + 23 = 47 chromosomes, which is a trisomy (e.g. trisomy 21). A gamete with 22 chromosomes would give a monosomy with 45.

**A213.** A. Crossing over, independent assortment, random fertilisation
<!-- id: bio12a3-sex-5 -->

Explanation: Crossing over (prophase I), independent assortment (metaphase I) and the random fusion of gametes at fertilisation all add genetic variation in sexual reproduction. Mitosis, binary fission and budding produce genetically identical cells.

**A214.** Crossing over: during prophase I homologous chromosomes pair and non-sister chromatids exchange segments at chiasmata; this produces recombinant chromatids with new combinations of alleles on the same chromosome. Independent assortment: during metaphase I each homologous pair lines up at the equator in a random orientation independent of other pairs; so maternal and paternal chromosomes are distributed to gametes in many different combinations (2ⁿ).
<!-- id: bio12a3-sex-6 -->

Explanation:

**A215.** Genetic diversity means individuals carry different alleles, so some are more likely to have traits that let them survive a new selection pressure such as disease or climate change; these individuals survive and reproduce so the population persists; for example the Tasmanian devil has very low genetic diversity, which has made it highly vulnerable to devil facial tumour disease, which spreads between devils because their immune systems do not recognise the tumour cells as foreign.
<!-- id: bio12a3-sex-7 -->

Explanation:

**A216.** In meiosis I homologous chromosomes separate, so the two cells produced are haploid (n) although each chromosome still has two chromatids; in meiosis II sister chromatids separate, like mitosis; this produces four haploid cells, each chromosome with a single chromatid, and the ploidy does not change in meiosis II.
<!-- id: bio12a3-sex-8 -->

#### Reproductive cloning

**A217.** B. an egg cell whose nucleus has been removed (enucleated)
<!-- id: bio12a3-clone-1 -->

Explanation: In SCNT the donor nucleus goes into an enucleated egg cell. The egg is then stimulated (e.g. by an electric pulse) to start dividing. The nucleus has to be removed first so that the clone's nuclear DNA comes only from the donor.

**A218.** C. the Finn Dorset nucleus donor
<!-- id: bio12a3-clone-2 -->

Explanation: The nucleus carries almost all the genes, so Dolly's nuclear DNA matched the Finn Dorset donor, and she looked like a Finn Dorset. Her mitochondrial DNA came from the egg donor's cytoplasm. The surrogate mother only provided the uterus.

**A219.** B. genetically identical to each other but not identical to either parent
<!-- id: bio12a3-clone-3 -->

Explanation: The embryo started from fertilisation, so it has a new combination of alleles from both parents. The split embryos all come from that one zygote, so they are identical to each other (like identical twins) but not to either parent.

**A220.** B. Small pieces of tissue are grown on sterile nutrient media with plant hormones to form genetically identical plantlets
<!-- id: bio12a3-clone-4 -->

Explanation: In tissue culture, explants (often meristem tissue) are grown on sterile media with nutrients and plant hormones such as auxins and cytokinins. The resulting plantlets come from mitosis, so they are clones of the parent plant. No pollination is involved, and meristem cells are diploid.

**A221.** B. Cloning adds no new genetic diversity to a population that already has little
<!-- id: bio12a3-clone-5 -->

Explanation: Clones copy existing genotypes, so they do not raise the low genetic diversity that endangered populations usually suffer from. Cloned animals can generally reproduce, and not all clones have shortened lifespans.

**A222.** A somatic (body) cell is taken from the cow to be cloned and its nucleus extracted; an egg cell is taken from a donor cow and its nucleus removed (enucleated); the donor nucleus is inserted into (or the somatic cell fused with) the enucleated egg and an electric pulse or chemicals stimulate it to divide; the resulting embryo is grown in culture to an early embryo and implanted into the uterus of a surrogate cow, which gives birth to a calf genetically identical (in nuclear DNA) to the somatic cell donor.
<!-- id: bio12a3-clone-6 -->

Explanation:

**A223.** Gene expression is affected by the environment and by random events in development (e.g. X-chromosome inactivation in female cats determines coat patches), so the same genotype can give a different phenotype; the clone also has mitochondrial DNA from the egg donor and develops in a different uterine environment from the donor.
<!-- id: bio12a3-clone-7 -->

Explanation:

**A224.** Benefit: animals or plants with desirable traits (e.g. high milk yield, disease resistance) can be copied exactly and quickly; risk: herds or crops of clones have low genetic diversity, so a single disease or environmental change could devastate them (also accept low success rates and health problems in cloned animals, or ethical concerns about animal welfare).
<!-- id: bio12a3-clone-8 -->

#### Structural, physiological and behavioural adaptations

**A225.** B. A bearded dragon basking in the sun in the morning to raise its body temperature
<!-- id: bio12a3-adapt-1 -->

Explanation: A behavioural adaptation is something an organism does. Basking is a behaviour that warms an ectotherm. Thick fur and sunken stomata are structural adaptations, and producing concentrated urine is a physiological one.

**A226.** C. It is a structural adaptation that reduces heat gain from the environment and water loss
<!-- id: bio12a3-adapt-2 -->

Explanation: Body shape is a structural feature. A smaller SA:V ratio means less surface relative to volume, so less heat is absorbed from a hot environment and less water is lost across the surface. It does not increase heat loss, and it is not a behaviour or an internal process.

**A227.** A. Stomata mainly on the upper surface of floating leaves - allows gas exchange with the air
<!-- id: bio12a3-adapt-3 -->

Explanation: Floating leaves like water lily pads have stomata on the upper surface, which is the side in contact with air, so gas exchange can happen there. Thick cuticles, deep roots and sunken stomata are xerophyte features for dry conditions.

**A228.** B. aestivation
<!-- id: bio12a3-adapt-4 -->

Explanation: Aestivation is dormancy during hot, dry conditions. Australian water-holding frogs, for example, burrow and aestivate underground. Hibernation is long-term dormancy during cold winter conditions.

**A229.** B. 2 only
<!-- id: bio12a3-adapt-5 -->

Explanation: CAM photosynthesis is a physiological (biochemical) adaptation: stomata open at night, when it is cooler, to take up CO₂ and reduce water loss. Spines and root systems are structural adaptations.

**A230.** Structural adaptations are physical features of the body, e.g. the large ears of the bilby, which lose heat; physiological adaptations are internal functions or processes, e.g. the spinifex hopping mouse producing highly concentrated urine to conserve water; behavioural adaptations are actions an organism carries out, e.g. kangaroos licking their forearms so evaporation cools the blood in surface vessels.
<!-- id: bio12a3-adapt-6 -->

Explanation:

**A231.** A thick waxy cuticle on leaves is waterproof, so less water evaporates through the leaf surface; sunken stomata or stomata in hair-lined pits trap a layer of humid air, which reduces the water vapour concentration gradient and slows transpiration (also accept: reduced leaf surface area/needle-like leaves lower the area for water loss; vertically hanging leaves, as in eucalypts, reduce heat absorbed in the midday sun; rolled leaves enclose stomata).
<!-- id: bio12a3-adapt-7 -->

Explanation:

**A232.** Longer ears have a larger surface area with many blood vessels, which increases heat loss to the environment by radiation and convection; in hot climates rabbits with longer ears are more likely to avoid overheating, survive and reproduce, so the trait becomes more common, while in cooler regions shorter ears reduce heat loss.
<!-- id: bio12a3-adapt-8 -->

#### Adaptations to Australian environments

**A233.** B. seeds are released after fire, when competition is reduced and nutrients from ash are available
<!-- id: bio12a3-aus-1 -->

Explanation: Serotiny holds seeds in woody fruits until fire triggers their release. After a fire the ground is clear, light is plentiful, competition is low and ash adds nutrients, so seedlings have a better chance of establishing. Serotiny does not stop the parent plant from burning.

**A234.** B. epicormic buds
<!-- id: bio12a3-aus-2 -->

Explanation: Epicormic buds sit under the bark on trunks and branches and sprout after fire to regrow the canopy. Lignotubers are woody swellings at or below ground level that can also resprout, but they are not on the trunk.

**A235.** B. It produces very concentrated urine, obtains metabolic water from food and is nocturnal, staying in humid burrows during the day
<!-- id: bio12a3-aus-3 -->

Explanation: The hopping mouse has long loops of Henle and makes extremely concentrated urine. It gets water from seeds and from cellular respiration (metabolic water). By being nocturnal and sheltering in humid burrows it cuts evaporative water loss. Sweating would waste water.

**A236.** A. cluster (proteoid) roots that increase surface area and release compounds that free phosphorus from the soil
<!-- id: bio12a3-aus-4 -->

Explanation: Cluster (proteoid) roots are dense mats of fine rootlets. They have a large surface area and release carboxylates that make bound phosphate soluble, so the plant can take up more phosphorus from poor soils. Pneumatophores are a mangrove adaptation to waterlogged, low-oxygen mud.

**A237.** B. structural
<!-- id: bio12a3-aus-5 -->

Explanation: The grooves between the scales are a physical body feature, which makes this a structural adaptation. (The lizard may also behave in ways that help, such as standing on damp sand, but the grooves themselves are structural.)

**A238.** Structural: a dense network of blood vessels close to the skin of the forearms, which loses heat when licked; physiological: produces concentrated urine and can slow or pause embryo development (embryonic diapause) when conditions are poor; behavioural: rests in shade during the hottest part of the day and is mostly active at dawn, dusk and night, and licks forearms so evaporation cools the blood.
<!-- id: bio12a3-aus-6 -->

Explanation:

**A239.** Elastic tendons in the hind legs (e.g. the Achilles tendon) store energy on landing and release it in the next hop, so above a certain speed the energy cost of hopping barely rises with speed; this lets kangaroos travel long distances between scattered food and water sources with relatively little energy use.
<!-- id: bio12a3-aus-7 -->

Explanation:

**A240.** Vertically hanging leaves present their edge to the midday sun, so less light and heat are absorbed in the hottest part of the day, lowering leaf temperature and transpiration; the thick waxy cuticle is waterproof and reduces evaporation of water from the leaf surface.
<!-- id: bio12a3-aus-8 -->

#### Interdependencies and keystone species

**A241.** B. has a disproportionately large effect on ecosystem structure relative to its abundance
<!-- id: bio12a3-inter-1 -->

Explanation: A keystone species has an effect on community structure that is far larger than its numbers would suggest. Removing it causes big changes. Keystone species are often predators but do not have to be: ecosystem engineers and pollinators can also be keystone species.

**A242.** C. mutualism
<!-- id: bio12a3-inter-2 -->

Explanation: Both species benefit (+/+), so this is mutualism. In commensalism one species benefits and the other is unaffected (+/0). In parasitism one benefits at the other's expense (+/−).

**A243.** A. a trophic cascade
<!-- id: bio12a3-inter-3 -->

Explanation: A trophic cascade happens when a change at one trophic level (fewer predators) spreads through the food web: urchins increase and graze down the kelp. Biomagnification is the build-up of toxins up a food chain, which is a different process.

**A244.** C. parasitism
<!-- id: bio12a3-inter-4 -->

Explanation: Mistletoe takes water and minerals through haustoria while the host gets no benefit, so it is parasitism (+/−). Mistletoe is a hemiparasite because it still photosynthesises, but its relationship with the host is still parasitic. Its berries and nectar do support many birds and insects.

**A245.** B. Dingoes suppress fox and cat numbers, reducing predation pressure on small native mammals
<!-- id: bio12a3-inter-5 -->

Explanation: Dingoes kill and scare away foxes and cats (mesopredators). When dingoes are removed, mesopredators are released, they increase and they prey more heavily on small native mammals. This is mesopredator release, a form of trophic cascade.

**A246.** Intraspecific competition is competition for limited resources between individuals of the same species, e.g. male red kangaroos competing for mates, or eucalypt seedlings of one species competing for light; interspecific competition is competition between individuals of different species for the same resource, e.g. introduced European honeybees and native bees competing for nectar and tree hollows, or rabbits and native herbivores competing for grasses.
<!-- id: bio12a3-inter-6 -->

Explanation:

**A247.** Flying foxes pollinate the flowers of many native trees (e.g. eucalypts) as they feed on nectar; they disperse seeds over long distances, often tens of kilometres, which helps regenerate forests and maintain gene flow between fragmented patches; because many plant species depend on them and, in turn, many other animals depend on those plants, losing flying foxes would have a disproportionately large effect on forest structure.
<!-- id: bio12a3-inter-7 -->

Explanation:

**A248.** The skink population would decrease; grasshoppers are the skinks' food source, so less food would lead to lower survival and reproduction of skinks (and they may face greater predation pressure as kookaburras have less alternative prey).

#### Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander ecological knowledge

**A249.** B. small, cool, patchy fires lit at particular times of year, creating a mosaic of burnt and unburnt areas
<!-- id: bio12a3-ik-1 -->

Explanation: Cultural burning uses low-intensity, cool, patchy fires, often in the cooler months or early dry season, guided by Country, plants and animals. The resulting mosaic cuts fuel loads, protects fire-sensitive species and supports regrowth of food plants.

**A250.** B. It reduces fuel loads and provides refuges and a range of habitat ages for different species
<!-- id: bio12a3-ik-2 -->

Explanation: Patches of different ages break up continuous fuel, so the risk of large, intense wildfires goes down. They also give animals unburnt refuges to shelter in and fresh regrowth to feed on, which supports biodiversity.

**A251.** B. detailed observation of local indicators such as weather, flowering of plants and animal behaviour
<!-- id: bio12a3-ik-3 -->

Explanation: These calendars come from long-term observation of local environmental cues, such as winds, rain, plants flowering and animals breeding or migrating. They often recognise more than four seasons, and the cues signal when to hunt, gather or burn.

**A252.** B. used detailed knowledge of the short-finned eel's life cycle and seasonal movements to build sustainable aquaculture systems
<!-- id: bio12a3-ik-4 -->

Explanation: The Budj Bim system of channels, weirs and ponds was built to trap and hold short-finned eels (kooyang) during their seasonal migrations. It is among the world's oldest aquaculture systems and reflects detailed ecological knowledge. Budj Bim is World Heritage listed.

**A253.** C. Working with Traditional Owners combines long-term, place-based knowledge with scientific methods, for example in ranger programs and fire management
<!-- id: bio12a3-ik-5 -->

Explanation: Traditional knowledge is built from observing particular places over thousands of years. Combined with scientific monitoring in partnership with Traditional Owners (for example Indigenous ranger programs and savanna burning), it improves land and sea management. Using this knowledge needs consultation and respect for cultural and intellectual property.

**A254.** Cool, low-intensity burns at the right time remove understorey fuel such as dry grass and leaf litter, so later fires have less fuel and are less intense; burning in small patches creates a mosaic of different-aged vegetation that acts as firebreaks and gives animals refuges; regrowth after burning provides fresh food for herbivores and promotes germination of fire-adapted plants, maintaining a diversity of habitats and species.
<!-- id: bio12a3-ik-6 -->

Explanation:

**A255.** Example: in parts of south-eastern Australia the flowering of certain wattles signals that fish such as mullet are running or that eels are moving (accept other valid examples, e.g. flowering of a plant indicating turtles or dugong are fat and ready to hunt); such indicators are reliable because both species respond to the same seasonal environmental cues (e.g. temperature, day length, rainfall), so their life cycle events happen at consistent times relative to each other.
<!-- id: bio12a3-ik-7 -->

Explanation:

**A256.** Knowledge of breeding seasons, feeding grounds and population changes guides when and where hunting occurs, avoiding times when animals are breeding; community-based management plans and cultural rules limit how many animals are taken and which individuals (e.g. avoiding pregnant females or young), helping keep populations stable.

### Unit 3 AOS 1: Nucleic acids and proteins

#### Nucleic acid structure

**A257.** B. A phosphate group, a deoxyribose sugar and a nitrogenous base
<!-- id: bio34a1-nas-1 -->

Explanation: A DNA nucleotide consists of a phosphate group, the pentose sugar deoxyribose and one of four nitrogenous bases (A, T, C or G). Ribose is the sugar in RNA nucleotides, not DNA.

**A258.** B. 28%
<!-- id: bio34a1-nas-2 -->

Explanation: By complementary base pairing, G = C = 22%, so G + C = 44%. The remaining 56% is A + T, and since A = T, thymine is 28%. 56% is the combined A + T value, not thymine alone.

**A259.** C. phosphodiester bonds between the sugar of one nucleotide and the phosphate of the next.
<!-- id: bio34a1-nas-3 -->

Explanation: The sugar-phosphate backbone is held together by covalent phosphodiester bonds linking the 3′ carbon of one sugar to the 5′ phosphate of the next nucleotide. Hydrogen bonds join the two strands to each other, not nucleotides within a strand.

**A260.** A. The base uracil
<!-- id: bio34a1-nas-4 -->

Explanation: RNA contains uracil in place of thymine. Both DNA and RNA have a sugar-phosphate backbone, contain cytosine and are built in the 5′ to 3′ direction.

**A261.** D. Fragment X separates into single strands at a higher temperature because G–C pairs form three hydrogen bonds.
<!-- id: bio34a1-nas-5 -->

Explanation: G–C pairs are held by three hydrogen bonds while A–T pairs are held by two, so DNA rich in G–C needs more energy (a higher temperature) to separate. Length is equal here, so base composition decides it.

**A262.** The two strands run in opposite directions; one strand runs 5′ to 3′ while its complementary strand runs 3′ to 5′ (the 5′ phosphate end of one strand lies opposite the 3′ hydroxyl end of the other).
<!-- id: bio34a1-nas-6 -->

Explanation:

**A263.** Complementary strand: 3′-TACGGCAA-5′ (equivalently written 5′-AACGGCAT-3′); the strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases.
<!-- id: bio34a1-nas-7 -->

Explanation:

**A264.** Both are single-stranded RNA polymers made of ribonucleotides containing ribose and A, U, C, G; mRNA is a linear molecule that carries a copy of the genetic instructions (codons) from the DNA to the ribosome; tRNA folds into a cloverleaf shape held by internal base pairing, carries an anticodon at one end and a specific amino acid at the 3′ end, delivering amino acids to the ribosome during translation.

#### Transcription and RNA processing

**A265.** B. binds to the promoter and synthesises RNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction using the template strand.
<!-- id: bio34a1-trp-1 -->

Explanation: RNA polymerase binds to the promoter upstream of the gene, unwinds the DNA and adds complementary ribonucleotides to the 3′ end of the growing RNA, reading the template strand 3′ to 5′. Start codons are recognised by ribosomes in translation, not by RNA polymerase.

**A266.** C. 5′-AUGCCUAAG-3′
<!-- id: bio34a1-trp-2 -->

Explanation: mRNA is complementary and antiparallel to the template strand, with U replacing T: template 3′-TACGGATTC-5′ gives mRNA 5′-AUGCCUAAG-3′. Option A contains thymine, so it is DNA, not RNA.

**A267.** A. Addition of a poly-A tail
<!-- id: bio34a1-trp-3 -->

Explanation: A poly-A tail (a long chain of adenine nucleotides) is added to the 3′ end. The methyl-guanine cap is added to the 5′ end, not the 3′ end. Splicing removes introns, not exons.

**A268.** D. It may contain different combinations of exons in different cells due to alternative splicing.
<!-- id: bio34a1-trp-4 -->

Explanation: Introns are removed and exons are joined. Through alternative splicing, some exons may be included or excluded, so one gene can produce several mature mRNAs and therefore several proteins. Removing introns usually makes the mature mRNA much shorter than the gene.

**A269.** B. introns, which make up most of the gene, were removed from the pre-mRNA during splicing.
<!-- id: bio34a1-trp-5 -->

Explanation: Eukaryotic genes often contain long introns that are transcribed into pre-mRNA and then spliced out, leaving only the exons in mature mRNA. Translation occurs in the cytoplasm, not before export, and only the template strand is ever transcribed.

**A270.** A 5′ methyl-guanine (methylated G) cap is added to the 5′ end, which protects the mRNA and helps ribosome attachment; a poly-A tail is added to the 3′ end, which protects the mRNA from degradation and aids export; introns are removed and exons are joined together by spliceosomes (splicing).
<!-- id: bio34a1-trp-6 -->

Explanation:

**A271.** During splicing, different combinations of exons from the same pre-mRNA can be retained or removed; this produces different mature mRNAs from one gene, each translated into a different polypeptide, so the number of proteins exceeds the number of genes.
<!-- id: bio34a1-trp-7 -->

Explanation:

**A272.** Prokaryotes lack a membrane-bound nucleus, so ribosomes can access mRNA as soon as it is made (eukaryotes separate transcription in the nucleus from translation in the cytoplasm); as a consequence, prokaryotic mRNA generally does not undergo splicing, capping and tailing, whereas eukaryotic pre-mRNA is processed in the nucleus before export.

**A273.** C. most amino acids are coded for by more than one codon.

#### Gene regulation and the trp operon

**A281.** A. corepressor that binds to the repressor protein, allowing it to bind the operator.
<!-- id: bio34a1-gro-1 -->

Explanation: When tryptophan levels are high, tryptophan binds to the inactive repressor, changing its shape so that it binds the operator and blocks RNA polymerase. Removing a repressor in response to a molecule describes an inducible operon such as lac, not trp.

**A282.** B. Regulatory genes code for proteins such as repressors that control the expression of structural genes.
<!-- id: bio34a1-gro-2 -->

Explanation: Regulatory genes (such as trpR) code for proteins that switch other genes on or off. Structural genes (such as trpE–trpA) code for proteins with other cell functions, such as enzymes. Regulatory genes are transcribed to produce their proteins.

**A283.** C. the structural genes continue to be transcribed even though tryptophan is abundant.
<!-- id: bio34a1-gro-3 -->

Explanation: Without bound tryptophan, the repressor stays inactive and cannot bind the operator, so RNA polymerase continues to transcribe the structural genes (attenuation may reduce, but not fully stop, transcription). The repressor could only bind the operator when tryptophan is bound.

**A284.** D. the ribosome moves quickly through the leader sequence, allowing a terminator hairpin (regions 3–4) to form so transcription stops.
<!-- id: bio34a1-gro-4 -->

Explanation: With plenty of tryptophan-carrying tRNA, the ribosome passes the two trp codons rapidly and covers region 2, so regions 3 and 4 pair to form a terminator hairpin and RNA polymerase detaches. Stalling and antiterminator formation occur when tryptophan is low.

**A285.** A. the added tryptophan acted as a corepressor and also enabled attenuation, both reducing transcription of the structural genes.
<!-- id: bio34a1-gro-5 -->

Explanation: Added tryptophan binds the repressor so it blocks the operator, and high tryptophan also causes early termination by attenuation; both reduce transcription quickly. Genes are not deleted and the repressor is a protein coded by trpR, not made from tryptophan.

**A286.** When tryptophan is low, no tryptophan binds to the repressor (coded by trpR), so the repressor stays inactive and cannot bind to the operator; RNA polymerase can therefore bind to the promoter and transcribe the structural genes that code for the enzymes that synthesise tryptophan.
<!-- id: bio34a1-gro-6 -->

Explanation:

**A287.** Making tryptophan-synthesis enzymes requires energy and resources (ATP, amino acids, nucleotides); by switching off the operon when tryptophan is already available, the bacterium avoids wasting resources and can direct them to other processes such as growth and reproduction.

#### Protein structure and the proteome

**A289.** C. the sequence of amino acids in the polypeptide chain.
<!-- id: bio34a1-psp-1 -->

Explanation: Primary structure is the linear sequence of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. α-helices and β-pleated sheets are secondary structure, 3D shape is tertiary structure and multiple polypeptides is quaternary structure.

**A290.** B. Hydrogen bonds between atoms of the polypeptide backbone
<!-- id: bio34a1-psp-2 -->

Explanation: α-helices and β-pleated sheets are stabilised by hydrogen bonds between the C=O and N–H groups of the backbone. Disulfide bridges and other R-group interactions are mainly associated with tertiary structure.

**A291.** D. Quaternary
<!-- id: bio34a1-psp-3 -->

Explanation: Quaternary structure is the association of two or more polypeptide chains into one functional protein. Myoglobin is a single polypeptide, so its highest level is tertiary structure.

**A292.** A. the complete set of proteins expressed by a cell, tissue or organism at a particular time.
<!-- id: bio34a1-psp-4 -->

Explanation: The proteome is the full set of proteins expressed by a cell, tissue or organism at a given time under given conditions. The complete set of genes is the genome. Unlike the genome, the proteome varies between cell types and over time.

**A293.** C. The bacterial enzyme has more stabilising bonds (such as disulfide bridges and ionic bonds) maintaining its tertiary structure.
<!-- id: bio34a1-psp-5 -->

Explanation: Heat disrupts the weaker bonds that hold tertiary structure, changing the active site (denaturation). Extra stabilising interactions help the bacterial enzyme keep its shape. Peptide bonds are not broken by denaturation, and the genetic code is universal.

**A294.** A condensation reaction occurs between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next; a molecule of water is released and a covalent peptide bond (C–N) forms between them.

**A296.** Different genes are switched on (expressed) or off in different cell types due to gene regulation; in addition, alternative splicing and post-translational modification can differ between cells, so each cell type produces a different set of proteins.

### Unit 3 AOS 1: DNA manipulation

#### Enzymes for DNA manipulation

**A305.** B. sticky ends with single-stranded 5′-AATT overhangs.
<!-- id: bio34a1-edm-1 -->

Explanation: Cutting G^AATTC on both antiparallel strands leaves staggered cuts with complementary 5′-AATT single-stranded overhangs (sticky ends). Blunt ends occur when both strands are cut at the same position, e.g. SmaI.

**A306.** C. Reverse transcriptase
<!-- id: bio34a1-edm-2 -->

Explanation: Reverse transcriptase synthesises complementary DNA (cDNA) from an mRNA template. Since mature mRNA has had its introns removed, the cDNA lacks introns, which is useful for expressing human genes in bacteria.

**A307.** D. join DNA fragments by forming phosphodiester bonds in the sugar-phosphate backbone.
<!-- id: bio34a1-edm-3 -->

Explanation: DNA ligase catalyses formation of phosphodiester bonds between adjacent nucleotides, sealing the backbone of joined fragments. Cutting at recognition sites is done by restriction enzymes; copying is done by DNA polymerase.

**A308.** A. Linear: 4 fragments; circular: 3 fragments
<!-- id: bio34a1-edm-4 -->

Explanation: Cutting a linear molecule n times produces n + 1 fragments (4), whereas cutting a circular molecule n times produces n fragments (3), because there are no free ends to begin with.

**A309.** B. Using the same enzyme produces complementary sticky ends that can base pair with each other.
<!-- id: bio34a1-edm-5 -->

Explanation: Cutting both with the same restriction enzyme produces complementary sticky ends, so the gene and plasmid ends can base pair by hydrogen bonding before ligase seals the backbone. Restriction enzymes do not join DNA.

**A310.** DNA polymerase synthesises a new DNA strand by adding free nucleotides one at a time, complementary to a template strand, to the 3′ end of a growing strand/primer; DNA ligase joins two existing DNA fragments together by forming a phosphodiester bond between them, and does not use a template.
<!-- id: bio34a1-edm-6 -->

Explanation:

**A311.** Restriction enzymes evolved as a defence against bacteriophages, cutting invading viral DNA at specific recognition sites; the bacteria's own DNA is protected because its recognition sites are chemically modified (methylated), so the enzyme cannot cut them.

Explanation:

**A312.** Probability = (1/4)⁶ = 1/4096, so on average once every 4096 bp; a four-base-pair site has a probability of (1/4)⁴ = 1/256, occurring once every 256 bp on average; it therefore occurs more often, cutting the DNA at more sites and producing more, smaller fragments.

#### CRISPR-Cas9

**A313.** C. provide an adaptive defence against bacteriophages by recognising and cutting viral DNA.
<!-- id: bio34a1-cas-1 -->

Explanation: CRISPR-Cas is a bacterial immune system: fragments of phage DNA are stored as spacers in the CRISPR array, and on reinfection the transcribed crRNA guides Cas9 to cut the matching viral DNA. It is not involved in operon regulation or conjugation.

**A314.** D. base pair with a complementary target DNA sequence and direct Cas9 to that site.
<!-- id: bio34a1-cas-2 -->

Explanation: The sgRNA contains a spacer sequence (about 20 nucleotides) complementary to the target DNA and binds Cas9, guiding it to the correct location. Cas9 is the endonuclease that cuts DNA; repair is done by the cell.

**A315.** A. protospacer adjacent motif (PAM).
<!-- id: bio34a1-cas-3 -->

Explanation: Cas9 must recognise a PAM (5′-NGG-3′ for S. pyogenes Cas9) adjacent to the target before it cuts. This prevents bacteria from cutting the spacers in their own CRISPR array, which lack PAMs.

**A316.** B. small insertions or deletions that can shift the reading frame and knock out the gene.
<!-- id: bio34a1-cas-4 -->

Explanation: NHEJ joins broken ends without a template and is error-prone, often adding or deleting a few nucleotides. These indels can cause a frameshift and a non-functional protein (a gene knockout). Precise insertion requires homology-directed repair with a template.

**A317.** D. Cas9 will not cut the target, even though the sgRNA is complementary, because no PAM is present.
<!-- id: bio34a1-cas-5 -->

Explanation: Cas9 requires a PAM adjacent to the target sequence to cut. Without one, no double-stranded break is made at that site. Cas9 does not cut randomly; it is directed by sgRNA complementarity plus the PAM.

**A318.** When a bacteriophage injects its DNA, Cas proteins (Cas1 and Cas2) cut out a short segment of the viral DNA; this segment is inserted as a new spacer into the bacterium's CRISPR array between repeat sequences; on a later infection the array is transcribed into crRNA, which guides Cas9 to the matching viral DNA so it is cut and destroyed.
<!-- id: bio34a1-cas-6 -->

Explanation:

**A319.** After Cas9 makes a double-stranded break at the target site, scientists supply a DNA template containing the desired sequence flanked by regions homologous to either side of the break; the cell uses homology-directed repair to copy the template into the break, inserting the corrected sequence.
<!-- id: bio34a1-cas-7 -->

Explanation:

**A320.** Advantage: it is cheaper, faster and more precise/easily targeted, since only the sgRNA sequence needs to be changed to target a new gene; concern: off-target cuts could cause harmful mutations, and edits to embryos are heritable (germline) and passed on to future generations without their consent.

**A321.** C. Denaturation 95 °C; annealing 55 °C; extension 72 °C

**A329.** D. are small circular DNA molecules that replicate independently and can carry inserted genes.
<!-- id: bio34a1-rpt-1 -->

**A331.** B. have taken up a plasmid, which may or may not contain the gene of interest.

### Unit 3 AOS 2: Biochemical pathways

#### Enzymes and coenzymes

**A345.** D. The active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds, forming a tighter enzyme–substrate complex
<!-- id: bio34a2-enz-1 -->

Explanation: In the induced fit model the active site is complementary but flexible, moulding around the substrate as it binds. The rigid, exact-match idea describes the older lock-and-key model, and enzymes are not consumed or permanently changed.

**A346.** A. lowering the activation energy of the reaction
<!-- id: bio34a2-enz-2 -->

Explanation: Enzymes are biological catalysts that provide an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy. They do not supply energy and do not change the final position of equilibrium; they only speed up attainment of it.

**A347.** C. NADP⁺ → NADPH, produced in the light-dependent stage of photosynthesis
<!-- id: bio34a2-enz-3 -->

Explanation: NADP⁺ accepts electrons and a proton to become NADPH in the light-dependent stage, and NADPH is unloaded in the Calvin cycle. NADH is made in cellular respiration, not the light-independent stage, and the Calvin cycle uses ATP rather than producing it.

**A348.** B. a non-protein organic molecule that assists an enzyme, often by carrying electrons, protons or chemical groups between reactions
<!-- id: bio34a2-enz-4 -->

Explanation: Coenzymes are organic, non-protein molecules (e.g. NAD⁺, NADP⁺, FAD, ATP/ADP) that bind temporarily and shuttle electrons, protons or phosphate groups between reactions. Inorganic helpers such as Mg²⁺ are cofactors but not coenzymes.

**A349.** D. B
<!-- id: bio34a2-enz-5 -->

Explanation: E2 converts B into C. Without functional E2, B cannot be converted, so B builds up while C and D are depleted. A still converts to B because E1 is functional.

**A350.** Enzymes are proteins whose tertiary structure is determined by the sequence of amino acids (primary structure); folding produces an active site with a specific three-dimensional shape and chemical properties (charge, hydrophobicity); only a substrate with a complementary shape and chemistry can bind to the active site to form an enzyme–substrate complex, so each enzyme is specific to one substrate/reaction.
<!-- id: bio34a2-enz-6 -->

Explanation:

**A351.** Energy released from catabolic reactions such as cellular respiration is used to add an inorganic phosphate (Pi) to ADP, forming ATP; ATP diffuses to sites where energy is needed; hydrolysis of ATP to ADP + Pi releases energy that drives anabolic or energy-requiring processes such as active transport, muscle contraction or protein synthesis; ADP + Pi are then recycled back to respiration to be reloaded.
<!-- id: bio34a2-enz-7 -->

Explanation:

**A352.** Unloaded coenzymes (NAD⁺) accept electrons and hydrogen ions released during glycolysis and the Krebs cycle, becoming loaded (NADH); loaded NADH carries these electrons and protons to the electron transport chain on the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it is unloaded (returning to NAD⁺) and the energy is used to produce ATP.
<!-- id: bio34a2-enz-8 -->

#### Factors affecting enzymes and inhibition

**A353.** C. non-competitive inhibitor
<!-- id: bio34a2-inh-1 -->

Explanation: Binding at a site other than the active site (an allosteric site) and altering active site shape is non-competitive inhibition. Competitive inhibitors and substrate analogues bind the active site itself.

**A354.** A. the enzyme was denatured, permanently changing the shape of its active site
<!-- id: bio34a2-inh-2 -->

Explanation: High temperatures break the hydrogen bonds and other interactions holding the tertiary structure, denaturing the enzyme. The change is irreversible here, so cooling does not restore activity. Peptide bonds (primary structure) are not broken by denaturation.

**A355.** D. Enzyme concentration (all active sites were occupied)
<!-- id: bio34a2-inh-3 -->

Explanation: At the plateau all active sites are saturated, so adding substrate has no effect; the rate is limited by the number of enzyme molecules. Adding more enzyme provides more active sites and raises the maximum rate, confirming enzyme concentration was limiting.

**A356.** B. increasing the concentration of substrate
<!-- id: bio34a2-inh-4 -->

Explanation: Competitive inhibitors compete with the substrate for the active site. Raising substrate concentration increases the likelihood that substrate, rather than inhibitor, occupies the active site. This does not overcome non-competitive inhibition.

**A357.** C. the change in pH alters the charges on amino acid R groups, disrupting the bonds that maintain the active site shape
<!-- id: bio34a2-inh-5 -->

Explanation: Changes in pH alter the ionisation of R groups, disrupting ionic and hydrogen bonds that hold the tertiary structure, so the active site loses its complementary shape. Each enzyme has an optimum pH matched to its environment.

**A358.** An irreversible inhibitor forms strong (often covalent) bonds with the enzyme, permanently inactivating it; a reversible inhibitor binds with weak bonds and can detach, so enzyme activity can be restored; because the insect's acetylcholinesterase is permanently inactivated, enzyme activity cannot recover (new enzyme must be synthesised), so nerve function fails and the insect dies.
<!-- id: bio34a2-inh-6 -->

Explanation:

**A359.** At 10 °C the enzyme and substrate molecules have low kinetic energy, so there are fewer successful collisions between substrate and active site and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form per unit time, giving low activity; the enzyme is not denatured at 10 °C, so activity would increase if warmed; at 60 °C heat disrupts the hydrogen bonds maintaining tertiary structure, so the enzyme denatures, the active site loses its shape and substrate can no longer bind, so activity is near zero.
<!-- id: bio34a2-inh-7 -->

Explanation:

**A360.** The final product of a pathway binds to (often allosterically inhibits) an enzyme early in the pathway, reducing its activity when product levels are high; this prevents over-production of the product and avoids wasting substrate and energy, with the pathway resuming when product levels fall.

#### Photosynthesis

**A361.** A. thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast
<!-- id: bio34a2-ps-1 -->

Explanation: Chlorophyll and the electron transport proteins of the light-dependent stage are embedded in the thylakoid membranes (stacked into grana). The stroma is the site of the light-independent stage (Calvin cycle).

**A362.** D. Inputs: CO₂, NADPH, ATP. Outputs: glucose, NADP⁺, ADP + Pi
<!-- id: bio34a2-ps-2 -->

Explanation: The Calvin cycle fixes CO₂ using energy from ATP and electrons/protons from NADPH, producing glucose and returning NADP⁺ and ADP + Pi to the light-dependent stage. The option with H₂O, NADP⁺ and ADP + Pi as inputs describes the light-dependent stage.

**A363.** D. Oxygen gas released from the leaf
<!-- id: bio34a2-ps-3 -->

Explanation: In the light-dependent stage, water is split (photolysis) to provide electrons and H⁺; the oxygen atoms from water are released as O₂. The oxygen atoms in glucose come from CO₂.

**A364.** A. the supply of ATP and NADPH from the light-dependent stage stops
<!-- id: bio34a2-ps-4 -->

Explanation: The Calvin cycle does not use light directly, but it depends on ATP and NADPH from the light-dependent stage. In darkness these are no longer produced, so the light-independent reactions slow and stop once existing supplies are used.

**A365.** C. CO₂ concentration
<!-- id: bio34a2-ps-5 -->

Explanation: At the plateau, increasing light has no effect, so light is not limiting. Because adding CO₂ increased the rate, CO₂ concentration was the factor limiting the Calvin cycle at that point.

**A366.** Rubisco is the enzyme in the stroma that catalyses carbon fixation, combining CO₂ with a 5-carbon compound (RuBP) at the start of the Calvin cycle; like other enzymes it has an optimum temperature; below the optimum, fewer successful collisions occur so the rate is slower, and above the optimum the enzyme begins to denature (and binds O₂ more readily), reducing the rate of carbon fixation.

**A367.** 6CO₂ + 12H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O (in the presence of light and chlorophyll; 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ also accepted); water is split in the light-dependent stage in the thylakoid membranes (grana); carbon dioxide is fixed in the light-independent stage (Calvin cycle) in the stroma.

Explanation:

#### C3, C4 and CAM plants

**A369.** B. rubisco binds oxygen instead of carbon dioxide, reducing the efficiency of carbon fixation
<!-- id: bio34a2-c4-1 -->

Explanation: Rubisco can bind O₂ as well as CO₂. When O₂ binds, energy and fixed carbon are wasted and CO₂ is released, lowering photosynthetic efficiency. PEP carboxylase does not bind O₂, which is why C4 plants use it.

**A370.** D. spatially separating initial CO₂ fixation in mesophyll cells from the Calvin cycle in bundle sheath cells
<!-- id: bio34a2-c4-2 -->

Explanation: In C4 plants PEP carboxylase fixes CO₂ into a 4-carbon compound in mesophyll cells; this is moved to bundle sheath cells, where CO₂ is released at high concentration around rubisco. Separating stages in time (night/day) describes CAM plants.

**A371.** C. CO₂ is fixed into a 4-carbon acid at night and released to rubisco for the Calvin cycle during the day
<!-- id: bio34a2-c4-3 -->

Explanation: Pineapple is a CAM plant. At night, PEP carboxylase fixes CO₂ into organic acids (malate) stored in vacuoles. During the day, with stomata closed, CO₂ is released internally and enters the Calvin cycle, which needs ATP and NADPH from light.

**A372.** A. A hot, arid desert with very low water availability
<!-- id: bio34a2-c4-4 -->

Explanation: CAM plants keep stomata closed during the hot day, greatly reducing water loss while still fixing carbon. This trade-off is most beneficial in hot, dry environments. In cool, moist conditions C3 plants are generally more efficient.

**A373.** D. at lower temperatures photorespiration in C3 plants is low, so the extra ATP cost of the C4 pathway is not offset
<!-- id: bio34a2-c4-5 -->

Explanation: Concentrating CO₂ in C4 plants costs extra ATP. When it is cool, rubisco binds O₂ less often and stomata can stay open, so C3 plants lose little to photorespiration and avoid this energy cost.

**A374.** In hot, dry conditions C3 plants close their stomata to reduce water loss by transpiration; this prevents CO₂ from diffusing into the leaf while O₂ produced by the light-dependent stage builds up, so the ratio of O₂ to CO₂ around rubisco increases; rubisco therefore binds O₂ more often (and its affinity for O₂ relative to CO₂ increases at higher temperatures), increasing photorespiration and reducing glucose production.
<!-- id: bio34a2-c4-6 -->

Explanation:

**A375.** Similarity: both first fix CO₂ using PEP carboxylase (which does not bind O₂) into a 4-carbon compound, later releasing CO₂ to rubisco for the Calvin cycle; difference: C4 plants separate the two steps spatially (mesophyll and bundle sheath cells) during the day, whereas CAM plants separate them in time (CO₂ fixed at night, Calvin cycle during the day) within the same cell.

#### Cellular respiration

**A377.** B. cytosol
<!-- id: bio34a2-cr-1 -->

#### Innate immunity

**A409.** D. It is non-specific, responds rapidly and does not produce immunological memory
<!-- id: bio34a2-inn-1 -->

Explanation: The innate response (second line of defence) responds to general features shared by many pathogens, acts within minutes to hours and does not create memory. Specificity and memory are features of adaptive immunity carried out by lymphocytes.

**A410.** C. cause vasodilation and increased permeability of capillaries
<!-- id: bio34a2-inn-2 -->

**A411.** A. cells that have abnormal or reduced MHC class I markers, such as virus-infected or cancerous cells

#### Antigen presentation and the lymphatic system

**A417.** C. all nucleated body cells
<!-- id: bio34a2-ap-1 -->

Explanation: MHC I markers are present on all nucleated cells and display fragments of proteins made inside the cell (self or, if infected, viral peptides). MHC II is restricted to antigen-presenting cells such as dendritic cells, macrophages and B cells. Mature red blood cells lack a nucleus and MHC I.

**A418.** A. present antigen fragments on MHC II to T helper cells with complementary receptors

Explanation: Dendritic cells are antigen-presenting cells that carry antigen through lymphatic vessels to lymph nodes, where many naive T cells are concentrated. There, a T helper cell with a complementary receptor binds the MHC II–antigen complex and is activated.

**A419.** D. Returning excess tissue fluid to the blood and providing sites where lymphocytes encounter antigens
<!-- id: bio34a2-ap-3 -->

Explanation: The lymphatic system collects excess tissue fluid (lymph) and returns it to the bloodstream, and its lymph nodes and spleen are secondary lymphoid tissues where lymphocytes are activated. It has no pump and does not make red blood cells.

**A420.** D. lymphocytes in the nodes are undergoing clonal expansion after encountering antigen
<!-- id: bio34a2-ap-4 -->

Explanation: When lymphocytes with receptors complementary to an antigen are activated in a lymph node, they proliferate rapidly (clonal expansion), increasing the size of the node. Swollen nodes are a sign that the adaptive response is under way.

**A421.** A. B cells in the bone marrow; T cells in the thymus
<!-- id: bio34a2-ap-5 -->

Explanation: Both B and T cells originate from stem cells in bone marrow. B cells mature in the bone marrow, while T cells migrate to and mature in the thymus. Both are primary lymphoid organs.

**A422.** MHC I is found on all nucleated cells, whereas MHC II is found only on antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells); MHC I presents peptides from proteins made inside the cell (endogenous, e.g. viral proteins in an infected cell), which are recognised by cytotoxic T cells; MHC II presents peptides from material engulfed from outside the cell (exogenous, e.g. phagocytosed bacteria), which are recognised by T helper cells.

#### Humoral immunity

**A425.** C. pathogens and antigens in extracellular fluids such as blood and lymph
<!-- id: bio34a2-hum-1 -->

Explanation: The humoral response produces antibodies that circulate in body fluids and act on extracellular pathogens and toxins. Infected and cancerous cells are targeted by cell-mediated immunity (cytotoxic T cells).

**A426.** B. a T helper cell recognises the antigen presented on the B cell's MHC II and releases cytokines
<!-- id: bio34a2-hum-2 -->

Explanation: The B cell internalises the antigen and presents fragments on MHC II. A T helper cell with a complementary receptor binds and releases cytokines (e.g. interleukins), stimulating the B cell to undergo clonal expansion and differentiation.

**A427.** D. variable region at the tips of the heavy and light chains
<!-- id: bio34a2-hum-3 -->

Explanation: Each antibody has two antigen-binding sites formed by the variable regions of a heavy and a light chain. Differences in amino acid sequence here give each antibody its specificity. The constant region determines the antibody class and its interactions with other immune components.

**A428.** C. neutralisation
<!-- id: bio34a2-hum-4 -->

Explanation: Neutralisation occurs when antibodies cover the parts of a pathogen or toxin needed to bind host cells. Agglutination is the clumping of pathogens by antibodies; opsonisation is coating pathogens to enhance phagocytosis.

**A429.** A. memory B cells produced during the first exposure rapidly differentiating into plasma cells
<!-- id: bio34a2-hum-5 -->

Explanation: During the primary response, clonal expansion produces memory B cells as well as plasma cells. On re-exposure, the larger population of memory B cells responds quickly, differentiating into plasma cells that produce more antibody, sooner.

**A430.** A naive B cell with B cell receptors (membrane-bound antibodies) complementary to the antigen binds it (clonal selection) and presents antigen fragments on MHC II; a T helper cell that has been activated against the same antigen binds the B cell and releases cytokines; the B cell is stimulated to undergo clonal expansion (rapid mitosis), producing many identical cells; these differentiate into plasma cells, which secrete large quantities of specific antibodies, and memory B cells, which persist and provide a faster secondary response.

**A431.** Agglutination: each antibody has (at least) two binding sites, so antibodies bind several bacteria at once, clumping them together so they cannot spread and are more easily engulfed by phagocytes; opsonisation: antibodies coat the bacteria and phagocytes recognise the constant region of bound antibodies, which increases the rate of phagocytosis.

### Unit 4 AOS 2: Change over time

#### Mutations, gene flow and genetic drift

**A457.** A. Mutation
<!-- id: bio34a3-mgd-1 -->

Explanation: Mutation changes DNA sequences and creates new alleles. Gene flow can introduce alleles that are new to a particular population, but they already existed elsewhere; drift and selection only change existing allele frequencies.

**A458.** C. causes a frameshift that alters every codon downstream.
<!-- id: bio34a3-mgd-2 -->

Explanation: Deleting one base shifts the reading frame, so all downstream codons are changed, often producing a non-functional protein or an early stop codon. A substitution affects at most one codon.

**A459.** C. a bottleneck effect.
<!-- id: bio34a3-mgd-3 -->

Explanation: A bottleneck occurs when a population is drastically reduced, so the surviving gene pool is a small, random sample of the original, reducing diversity. The founder effect involves a small group colonising a new area.

**A460.** B. A population of 25 rock-wallabies isolated on an island
<!-- id: bio34a3-mgd-4 -->

Explanation: Genetic drift is random change in allele frequency and has the greatest effect in small, isolated populations, where chance events strongly change the proportions of alleles passed on. Gene flow counteracts drift.

**A461.** D. gene flow, which makes the gene pools of the two populations more similar.
<!-- id: bio34a3-mgd-5 -->

Explanation: Movement of alleles between populations via gametes (pollen) is gene flow; it tends to make populations more genetically similar. No new mutation occurs in this process.

**A462.** Founder effect: a small group of individuals leaves a population to colonise a new area, so the new population's gene pool reflects only the founders' alleles; bottleneck effect: a large population is drastically reduced in size by a chance event (e.g. bushfire, disease), and survivors carry only a subset of alleles; similarity: both are forms of genetic drift that reduce genetic diversity and can change allele frequencies by chance.
<!-- id: bio34a3-mgd-6 -->

Explanation:

**A463.** Genetic drift (a bottleneck effect); because the deaths were random rather than related to phenotype, the 20 survivors happened by chance to carry fewer B alleles, so the allele frequency changed without selection.
<!-- id: bio34a3-mgd-7 -->

Explanation:

**A464.** A somatic mutation occurs in a body cell and is not passed to offspring, so it cannot change the population's gene pool; a germline mutation occurs in cells that produce gametes and can be inherited, introducing a new allele into the gene pool on which evolutionary mechanisms can act.
<!-- id: bio34a3-mgd-8 -->

#### Natural selection and selective breeding

**A465.** C. Individuals deliberately changing their traits to suit the environment

**A466.** B. longer-legged toads disperse faster, reach new areas first and breed with other fast toads.

Explanation: At the front, toads with heritable longer legs travel further and arrive first, so they breed together and pass on the alleles, increasing their frequency (spatial sorting/selection). Acquired characteristics are not inherited, and mutations do not arise in response to need.

**A467.** A. involves humans choosing which individuals reproduce, often reducing genetic diversity.
<!-- id: bio34a3-nss-3 -->

Explanation: In selective breeding, humans are the selecting agent and breed individuals with desired traits, which increases desired allele frequencies but narrows the gene pool, reducing diversity.

**A468.** D. predation by birds that see contrasting moths more easily.
<!-- id: bio34a3-nss-4 -->

Explanation: The selection pressure is the environmental factor that affects survival: birds preferentially eat moths that contrast with the bark. The dark colour is the advantageous phenotype, not the pressure.

**A469.** C. Plants are genetically very similar, so the whole crop may be vulnerable to a single new disease.
<!-- id: bio34a3-nss-5 -->

Explanation: Selective breeding narrows genetic diversity; with uniform genotypes, a pathogen able to infect one plant can infect most of the crop, risking total crop failure.

**A470.** Variation: snails in the population vary in shell thickness due to different alleles (arising from mutation); selection pressure: crabs crush and eat thin-shelled snails more easily; selective advantage: thick-shelled snails are more likely to survive and reproduce; inheritance: they pass the thick-shell alleles to offspring, so over many generations the frequency of the thick-shell allele/phenotype increases in the population.

Explanation:

**A471.** Humans apply a strong, consistent and deliberate selection pressure, allowing only individuals with the desired trait to breed each generation; in natural selection, pressures vary and individuals without the advantageous trait still often reproduce, so allele frequencies change more slowly.

**A472.** Genetic diversity decreases because only a few sheep with particular alleles are used for breeding (and inbreeding increases); consequence: the flock has a reduced ability to adapt to environmental change/disease, or recessive harmful conditions become more common due to increased homozygosity.

#### Antibiotic and antiviral resistance

**A473.** C. Resistant bacteria already present survive the antibiotic and reproduce, increasing the frequency of resistance.
<!-- id: bio34a3-aar-1 -->

Explanation: Resistance alleles arise by random mutation (or are acquired by horizontal gene transfer) before exposure; the antibiotic acts as a selection pressure, killing susceptible bacteria. Bacteria, not patients, become resistant.

**A474.** B. transfer of plasmids by conjugation.
<!-- id: bio34a3-aar-2 -->

Explanation: Horizontal gene transfer via plasmids (conjugation) spreads resistance genes between bacteria. Binary fission passes genes only to daughter cells (vertical transfer), and bacteria do not undergo meiosis.

**A475.** A. It allows the more-resistant bacteria that survived early doses to multiply.
<!-- id: bio34a3-aar-3 -->

Explanation: Early doses kill the most susceptible bacteria; stopping early leaves those with partial resistance alive to reproduce, so resistance alleles increase in frequency.

**A476.** D. increase, as the drug selects for viruses with the mutation.
<!-- id: bio34a3-aar-4 -->

Explanation: The drug is a selection pressure: susceptible viruses are inhibited while resistant variants replicate and spread, increasing their frequency. Viruses evolve rapidly because of high mutation rates and short replication times.

**A477.** C. Prescribing antibiotics only for bacterial infections, using narrow-spectrum drugs where possible
<!-- id: bio34a3-aar-5 -->

Explanation: Reducing unnecessary use lowers the selection pressure for resistance. Antibiotics are ineffective against viruses, and routine use in livestock and relying on one drug increase selection for resistance.

**A478.** Variation: some bacteria possess a resistance allele/gene that arose by random mutation or was acquired by plasmid transfer; selection pressure: methicillin use kills susceptible bacteria; resistant bacteria survive and reproduce rapidly by binary fission, passing on the resistance gene (and may share it by conjugation); over many generations the frequency of resistant bacteria increases until most of the population is resistant.
<!-- id: bio34a3-aar-6 -->

Explanation:

**A479.** For a virus to survive, it would need resistance mutations to all three drugs at once, which is extremely unlikely; so combination therapy reduces the chance that resistant variants survive and are selected, slowing the evolution of resistance.
<!-- id: bio34a3-aar-7 -->

Explanation:

**A480.** Bacteria have very short generation times (can divide every 20–30 minutes) and huge population sizes, so mutations arise frequently and selection acts over many generations quickly; humans have long generation times and fewer offspring, so changes in allele frequency take much longer.

#### Speciation

**A481.** D. can interbreed under natural conditions to produce viable, fertile offspring.
<!-- id: bio34a3-spe-1 -->

Explanation: The biological species concept is based on reproductive compatibility producing viable, fertile offspring. Similar appearance and shared habitat are not sufficient, and individuals within a species have different DNA sequences.

**A482.** A. Geographical isolation → different selection pressures and genetic drift → genetic divergence → reproductive isolation
<!-- id: bio34a3-spe-2 -->

Explanation: A geographical barrier prevents gene flow; each population experiences different selection pressures, mutations and drift, so gene pools diverge until the populations can no longer interbreed even if brought together.

**A483.** B. are separate species, as their hybrid offspring are not fertile.
<!-- id: bio34a3-spe-3 -->

Explanation: Mules are infertile, so horses and donkeys cannot produce fertile offspring and are separate species (post-zygotic isolation). Their ability to hybridise reflects a relatively recent common ancestor.

**A484.** C. temporal (seasonal) reproductive isolation.
<!-- id: bio34a3-spe-4 -->

Explanation: Breeding at different times prevents interbreeding even though the populations share a habitat; this is a pre-zygotic temporal isolating mechanism, which can lead to sympatric speciation.

**A485.** D. a polyploid plant cannot produce fertile offspring with the parent species, so it is instantly reproductively isolated.
<!-- id: bio34a3-spe-5 -->

Explanation: A polyploid (e.g. tetraploid) crossed with a diploid parent produces triploid offspring that are usually sterile because chromosomes cannot pair in meiosis, so the polyploid is reproductively isolated in the same location.

**A486.** Rising sea level created a geographical barrier, so gene flow between island and mainland populations stopped; the island population faced different selection pressures (e.g. climate, food, predators) and natural selection favoured different alleles; random mutations and genetic drift (small island population) further changed allele frequencies; over many generations the gene pools diverged so much that the populations would be reproductively isolated (unable to produce fertile offspring) if reunited, meaning a new species had formed (allopatric speciation).
<!-- id: bio34a3-spe-6 -->

Explanation:

**A487.** Allopatric speciation occurs when populations are separated by a geographical barrier that prevents gene flow; sympatric speciation occurs within the same geographical area, where reproductive isolation arises without a physical barrier (e.g. by polyploidy, behavioural or temporal isolation).
<!-- id: bio34a3-spe-7 -->

Explanation:

**A488.** Observe (or allow) the two populations to interbreed under natural conditions; if they do not mate or their offspring are not viable/fertile (e.g. test whether hybrid offspring can produce their own offspring), they are reproductively isolated and are separate species; if fertile offspring are produced, speciation has not occurred.

#### Fossil record and dating

**A489.** B. Rapid burial in fine sediment with low oxygen
<!-- id: bio34a3-frd-1 -->

Explanation: Rapid burial in fine sediment (e.g. mud, volcanic ash) in anoxic conditions protects remains from scavengers, decomposers and oxygen, allowing mineralisation. Oxygen and decomposers break remains down.

**A490.** B. Fossil X is older than fossil Y.
<!-- id: bio34a3-frd-2 -->

Explanation: The law of superposition states that, in undisturbed strata, lower layers were deposited earlier and are older. This is relative dating, which gives order but not an absolute age.

**A491.** C. 17 190 years.
<!-- id: bio34a3-frd-3 -->

Explanation: One-eighth remaining = (1/2)³, so three half-lives have passed: 3 × 5730 = 17 190 years. Two half-lives (11 460 years) would leave one-quarter.

**A492.** C. the half-life of carbon-14 is too short, so almost no carbon-14 would remain.
<!-- id: bio34a3-frd-4 -->

Explanation: Carbon-14 is useful up to about 50 000 years; after that too little remains to measure. Older fossils are dated by measuring isotopes with long half-lives (e.g. potassium-40 → argon-40) in volcanic layers above and below the fossil.

**A493.** A. they are formed by cyanobacteria, and fossil stromatolites are among the oldest evidence of life (about 3.5 billion years).
<!-- id: bio34a3-frd-5 -->

Explanation: Stromatolites are layered structures built by cyanobacterial mats trapping sediment; fossil examples from the Pilbara are about 3.5 billion years old, and living Shark Bay stromatolites help scientists interpret them.

**A494.** Fossilisation requires rare conditions (rapid burial, low oxygen), so most organisms decompose or are scavenged without forming fossils; soft-bodied organisms rarely fossilise; and many fossils are destroyed by erosion or geological activity, or have not yet been discovered. (Any two.)
<!-- id: bio34a3-frd-6 -->

Explanation:

**A495.** The volcanic ash layers were dated by absolute (radiometric) dating, e.g. potassium-argon dating, which measures the ratio of a parent isotope to its stable daughter product and uses the known half-life; sedimentary rock itself cannot easily be dated this way; by superposition, the fossil is younger than the lower layer and older than the upper layer, so it is between 3.2 and 3.6 million years old.

Explanation:

**A496.** An index fossil is from a species that was widespread geographically but existed for a relatively short period of time; if the same index fossil is found in rock layers at both sites, the layers can be inferred to be of similar age (relative dating/correlation).

#### Evidence of relatedness and phylogenetic trees

**A497.** C. homologous structures, showing divergent evolution from a common ancestor.
<!-- id: bio34a3-erp-1 -->

Explanation: Homologous structures share the same underlying structure inherited from a common ancestor but have been modified for different functions (divergent evolution). Analogous structures have similar functions but different origins.

**A498.** B. analogous structures.
<!-- id: bio34a3-erp-2 -->

Explanation: Bird and insect wings both enable flight but have entirely different structures and evolutionary origins, so they are analogous, resulting from convergent evolution under similar selection pressures.

**A499.** C. Humans share a more recent common ancestor with the rhesus monkey than with any of the other species listed.
<!-- id: bio34a3-erp-3 -->

Explanation: The fewer amino acid differences, the more recent the common ancestor. Rhesus monkey has only 1 difference. The data compare each species with humans, so they do not show how kangaroos relate to dogs.

**A500.** B. a common ancestor from which two or more lineages diverged.
<!-- id: bio34a3-erp-4 -->

Explanation: Each node (branch point) represents a hypothetical common ancestor where lineages split. The tips of branches represent the taxa being compared.

**A501.** D. DNA comparisons detect all changes, including silent mutations and non-coding regions that do not alter amino acids.
<!-- id: bio34a3-erp-5 -->

Explanation: Because the genetic code is degenerate, some DNA changes do not change the amino acid sequence; DNA also includes non-coding regions. DNA comparisons therefore reveal more differences and give finer resolution of relatedness.

**A502.** Vestigial structures are reduced structures that have little or no function in the organism but were functional in its ancestors; example: the human coccyx (tailbone), or pelvic bones in whales/pythons; they provide evidence that the species shares a common ancestor with organisms in which the structure is functional, having been retained from that ancestor but reduced through evolution as it lost its selective advantage.
<!-- id: bio34a3-erp-6 -->

Explanation:

**A503.** P and Q are most closely related (2 differences) and share the most recent common ancestor; R and S are more closely related to each other (4 differences) than to P or Q, forming a second group; the two groups (P+Q and R+S) diverged from an earlier common ancestor, at the base of the tree.
<!-- id: bio34a3-erp-7 -->

Explanation:

**A504.** The number of DNA (or amino acid) differences between two species is counted; dividing by the known mutation rate (calibrated using fossils of known age) estimates the time since they shared a common ancestor; limitation: mutation rates can vary between genes, species and over time (e.g. due to generation time or selection), so estimates may be inaccurate.

#### Human evolution

**A505.** B. A foramen magnum positioned centrally underneath the skull
<!-- id: bio34a3-hev-1 -->

Explanation: A centrally placed foramen magnum allows the skull to balance on top of a vertical spine, indicating bipedalism. In quadrupedal apes it is positioned further back. The other features relate to diet or general morphology.

**A506.** C. short, broad and bowl-shaped, supporting the internal organs and attachment of walking muscles.
<!-- id: bio34a3-hev-2 -->

Explanation: The human pelvis is short and bowl-shaped, supporting abdominal organs during upright posture and positioning muscles for efficient bipedal walking. Chimpanzees have a long, narrow pelvis suited to quadrupedal locomotion.

**A507.** B. Homo sapiens interbred with Neanderthals and Denisovans after migrating out of Africa.
<!-- id: bio34a3-hev-3 -->

Explanation: Shared DNA segments indicate interbreeding (gene flow) between Homo sapiens and archaic hominins in Eurasia after the out-of-Africa migration. Homo sapiens evolved in Africa, not from Neanderthals.

**A508.** C. 65 000 years.
<!-- id: bio34a3-hev-4 -->

Explanation: Artefacts at Madjedbebe have been dated to around 65 000 years ago, supporting the long, continuous presence of Aboriginal peoples in Australia. Homo sapiens has existed for only about 300 000 years.

**A509.** D. modern humans and extinct species more closely related to humans than to chimpanzees.
<!-- id: bio34a3-hev-5 -->

Explanation: Hominins are the group consisting of modern humans and extinct relatives on the human lineage after its split from the chimpanzee lineage (e.g. Australopithecus, Homo erectus). Hominids include all great apes.

**A510.** Valgus angle: the femurs angle inwards towards the knees, placing the feet under the body's centre of gravity for balance when walking; short, broad, bowl-shaped pelvis: supports the internal organs and provides attachment for muscles that stabilise the body on one leg during walking; (also accept S-shaped spine, which positions the centre of gravity over the hips and absorbs shock; or arched foot and non-opposable big toe in line with other toes, which act as a lever and absorb shock).
