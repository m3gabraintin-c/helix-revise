### Unit 1 AOS 1: How does the brain function?

#### Approaches to understanding the brain

**A1.** B. verbally name the object
<!-- id: psych12a1-appr-1 -->

Explanation: Information from the left visual field goes to the right hemisphere, and language production is usually lateralised to the left hemisphere. With the corpus callosum cut, the information cannot reach the left hemisphere, so the person cannot name it, though the left hand (right hemisphere) can still select it.

**A2.** D. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)
<!-- id: psych12a1-appr-2 -->

Explanation: fMRI measures the blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) signal and gives good spatial resolution. EEG measures electrical activity at the scalp with excellent temporal but poor spatial resolution; CT shows structure only.

**A3.** C. high temporal resolution
<!-- id: psych12a1-appr-3 -->

Explanation: EEG has high temporal resolution (milliseconds) but poor spatial resolution. Detailed anatomy is the strength of CT or MRI, and radioactive tracers are used in PET.

**A4.** C. a case study that suggested a link between the frontal lobe and personality
<!-- id: psych12a1-appr-4 -->

Explanation: Gage is a classic case study, which provides rich detail on one individual but limits generalisation. It suggested the frontal lobe is involved in personality and social behaviour; it was not an experiment.

**A5.** B. Findings may not generalise because the damage is unique to each individual
<!-- id: psych12a1-appr-5 -->

Explanation: Each injury differs in location, extent and the person's prior history, so results from one case may not generalise. Case studies do provide detailed, in-depth information.

**A6.** Similarity: both are non-invasive (do not require surgery) and can be used with healthy participants to study brain activity during tasks (1); Difference: fMRI has high spatial resolution because it detects blood oxygen changes in specific regions, whereas EEG has poor spatial resolution (1); EEG has high temporal resolution (milliseconds) whereas fMRI has lower temporal resolution because blood flow changes are slower (1).
<!-- id: psych12a1-appr-6 -->

Explanation:

**A7.** (a) The participant would say they saw 'key' (1); (b) The right visual field is processed by the left hemisphere, which controls speech, so the word 'key' can be named (1); the left visual field is processed by the right hemisphere, which lacks language production, and the corpus callosum cannot transfer the information so 'ring' cannot be verbalised (1).
<!-- id: psych12a1-appr-7 -->

Explanation:

**A8.** Technique: fMRI (or PET) (1); Strength: shows which specific regions have increased blood flow/oxygen use during the task, with good spatial resolution (1); Limitation: expensive, participant must lie still in a scanner, and poor temporal resolution so it cannot show rapid changes (1). Accept a justified alternative such as EEG if its strength and limitation are correct.

#### Structure and function of the brain

**A9.** A. Broca's area
<!-- id: psych12a1-stru-1 -->

Explanation: Broca's area, in the left frontal lobe, is involved in speech production. Damage to Wernicke's area typically results in fluent but meaningless speech and poor comprehension.

**A10.** B. Hippocampus
<!-- id: psych12a1-stru-2 -->

Explanation: The hippocampus is critical for forming new explicit (declarative) memories. The amygdala is associated with emotional responses such as fear and with emotional memories.

**A11.** B. parietal lobe
<!-- id: psych12a1-stru-3 -->

Explanation: The primary somatosensory cortex receives touch, temperature and pain information and lies in the parietal lobe. The primary motor cortex is in the frontal lobe.

**A12.** D. Cerebellum
<!-- id: psych12a1-stru-4 -->

Explanation: The cerebellum coordinates voluntary movement, balance and posture. Prefrontal cortex damage would be expected to affect planning and decision-making.

**A13.** D. The corpus callosum is a bundle of fibres that connects the hemispheres
<!-- id: psych12a1-stru-5 -->

Explanation: The corpus callosum allows communication between the hemispheres. Each hemisphere mainly controls the contralateral (opposite) side of the body, and language is usually lateralised to the left hemisphere.

**A14.** Accept two well-justified structures, e.g. temporal lobe (involved in recognising faces/auditory and object recognition) (1+1); amygdala (processes fear and emotional responses, so reduced fear suggests damage) (1+1). Each structure named (1) and linked to its function (1).
<!-- id: psych12a1-stru-6 -->

Explanation:

**A15.** Frontal lobe: involved in voluntary movement (motor cortex), planning, decision-making, reasoning, judgement and personality (1-2); Example: damage to the prefrontal cortex may lead to impulsive behaviour, poor judgement or changes in personality/emotional regulation (1).
<!-- id: psych12a1-stru-7 -->

Explanation:

**A16.** Hippocampus: forms new explicit/declarative memories and is involved in spatial memory (1); Amygdala: processes emotions, especially fear and aggression, and adds emotional significance to memories (1).

#### Neuroplasticity and adolescent brain development

**A17.** A. the elimination of weaker, rarely used synaptic connections
<!-- id: psych12a1-neur-1 -->

Explanation: Pruning removes less-used synapses to make neural networks more efficient. Strengthening through repeated use is long-term potentiation, and myelination is a separate process.

**A18.** B. a long-lasting strengthening of synaptic connections after repeated stimulation
<!-- id: psych12a1-neur-2 -->

Explanation: LTP is the long-lasting increase in synaptic strength that follows repeated activation, underlying learning and memory. The weakening of synapses with disuse is long-term depression (LTD).

**A19.** A. a prefrontal cortex that is still developing, with the limbic system relatively more active
<!-- id: psych12a1-neur-3 -->

Explanation: The prefrontal cortex (planning, impulse control) continues developing into the mid-20s, while the limbic system matures earlier. This helps explain greater risk-taking and emotional reactivity in adolescence.

**A20.** A. adaptive plasticity involving rerouting
<!-- id: psych12a1-neur-4 -->

Explanation: Adaptive plasticity after injury can involve rerouting, where neural connections are redirected around damaged areas, and sprouting of new connections. Pruning removes unused synapses and LTD weakens them.

**A21.** A. Neurons forming new dendrites and synaptic connections
<!-- id: psych12a1-neur-5 -->

Explanation: Sprouting is the growth of new nerve endings and synaptic connections. Elimination of unused connections is pruning; weakening of synapses is LTD.

**A22.** Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change its structure and function in response to experience (1); repeated practice repeatedly activates the same neural pathways, strengthening synaptic connections through long-term potentiation (1); new synaptic connections may form (sprouting) while unused connections weaken or are pruned, making the neural network more efficient so skills improve (1).
<!-- id: psych12a1-neur-6 -->

Explanation:

**A23.** Changes (any two): synaptic pruning of unused connections; myelination increasing the speed of neural transmission; ongoing development of the prefrontal cortex; limbic system maturing earlier (1 for two changes identified); Link: the prefrontal cortex, responsible for judgement and impulse control, is still developing while the limbic system (reward and emotion) is highly active, so adolescents may be more prone to risk-taking (2).
<!-- id: psych12a1-neur-7 -->

Explanation:

**A24.** LTP: long-lasting strengthening of synapses after frequent/repeated stimulation, making transmission more efficient (1); LTD: long-lasting weakening of synaptic connections after infrequent or low stimulation, which can lead to the connection being pruned (1).
<!-- id: psych12a1-neur-8 -->

#### Acquired brain injury and neurological disorders

**A25.** D. A stroke caused by a blocked artery
<!-- id: psych12a1-acqu-1 -->

Explanation: Stroke is a non-traumatic acquired brain injury, as it results from a blockage or bleed. The other options are traumatic brain injuries caused by external force.

**A26.** A. contralateral control of the body by each hemisphere
<!-- id: psych12a1-acqu-2 -->

Explanation: Each hemisphere mainly controls the opposite (contralateral) side of the body, so left motor cortex damage produces right-sided weakness.

**A27.** D. Dopamine
<!-- id: psych12a1-acqu-3 -->

Explanation: Parkinson's disease results from degeneration of dopamine-producing neurons, leading to tremor, rigidity and slowed movement.

**A28.** B. Difficulty forming new long-term memories
<!-- id: psych12a1-acqu-4 -->

Explanation: The hippocampus is critical for forming new explicit long-term memories. Speech production difficulty implies Broca's area, vision loss the occipital lobe, and balance the cerebellum.

**A29.** B. The effects depend on the location and severity of the injury
<!-- id: psych12a1-acqu-5 -->

Explanation: Effects depend on location and extent, and can include physical, cognitive, emotional and behavioural changes. Neuroplasticity can support partial recovery.

**A30.** Any two with justification: difficulty planning and making decisions; impulsivity or poor judgement; changes in personality or emotional regulation (e.g. irritability, aggression); reduced attention or problem solving; difficulty with voluntary movement (1 mark each).
<!-- id: psych12a1-acqu-6 -->

Explanation:

**A31.** Neuroplasticity allows the brain to reorganise after damage (1); undamaged areas can take over function through rerouting and sprouting new synaptic connections (1); rehabilitation such as physiotherapy, speech therapy or repeated practice of lost skills strengthens the new pathways (1).
<!-- id: psych12a1-acqu-7 -->

Explanation:

**A32.** Traumatic: caused by external physical force to the head, e.g. a fall or car accident (1+1 for definition and example); Non-traumatic: caused by internal factors such as a blockage, bleed, infection or lack of oxygen, e.g. stroke or tumour (1).

### Unit 1 AOS 2: What influences psychological development?

#### Nature, nurture and the biopsychosocial approach

**A33.** A. nature
<!-- id: psych12a1-natu-1 -->

Explanation: Nature refers to inherited genetic and biological factors. Nurture refers to environmental influences such as parenting, culture and experience.

**A34.** C. behaviour results from the interaction of biological, psychological and social factors
<!-- id: psych12a1-natu-2 -->

Explanation: The biopsychosocial approach treats biological, psychological and social factors as interacting influences on behaviour and mental processes.

**A35.** C. Pattern of negative thinking
<!-- id: psych12a1-natu-3 -->

Explanation: Psychological factors include thinking, emotion, coping and learning. Genetics and neurotransmitters are biological; peer influence is social.

**A36.** C. genetics
<!-- id: psych12a1-natu-4 -->

Explanation: Identical (monozygotic) twins share 100% of their genes, so similarity despite different environments suggests genetic influence. Twin studies cannot rule out all environmental effects.

**A37.** C. biological, psychological and social factors can interact
<!-- id: psych12a1-natu-5 -->

Explanation: Both biological predisposition and environmental stressors contributed, consistent with the biopsychosocial approach.

**A38.** Nature: genetic and biological factors that influence development, e.g. inherited capacity for language/brain structures (1); Nurture: environmental influences, e.g. exposure to language, interaction with caregivers (1); both interact, as a child needs both the biological capacity and exposure to language to develop speech (1).
<!-- id: psych12a1-natu-6 -->

Explanation:

**A39.** Biological: genetic predisposition, imbalance of neurotransmitters, or hormonal changes (1); Psychological: negative thought patterns, low self-esteem or poor coping skills (1); Social: bullying, family conflict, social isolation or lack of support (1).

Explanation:

**A40.** Identical twins raised together share both genes and environment, so it is hard to separate the two influences (1); twins raised apart are rare, giving small samples, and they may still share prenatal or similar environments, limiting generalisability (1). Accept another valid limitation.

#### Emotional development and attachment

**A41.** A. insecure-avoidant
<!-- id: psych12a1-emot-1 -->

Explanation: Insecure-avoidant infants show little distress at separation and avoid the caregiver on reunion. Insecure-resistant infants are very distressed at separation and are not comforted on return.

**A42.** B. uses the caregiver as a secure base, is distressed when they leave and is easily comforted on return
<!-- id: psych12a1-emot-2 -->

Explanation: Secure infants explore using the caregiver as a secure base, are upset when separated, and are quickly comforted when the caregiver returns.

**A43.** D. spent more time clinging to the cloth mother, suggesting contact comfort is important for attachment
<!-- id: psych12a1-emot-3 -->

Explanation: The monkeys spent most of their time with the cloth mother and went to the wire mother only to feed, showing that attachment is based on more than feeding. Note the ethical concerns raised by the study.

**A44.** A. Infant monkeys experienced significant psychological harm through deprivation
<!-- id: psych12a1-emot-4 -->

Explanation: The infant monkeys suffered long-term emotional harm from maternal deprivation, raising concerns about harm to animals.

**A45.** B. A caregiver who responds sensitively and consistently to the infant's needs
<!-- id: psych12a1-emot-5 -->

Explanation: Sensitive, consistent caregiving builds the infant's trust that needs will be met, which underpins secure attachment.

**A46.** Infant and caregiver are placed in an unfamiliar room with toys, with a series of episodes in which the caregiver and a stranger enter and leave, separating and reuniting the infant and caregiver (1+1); observers record the infant's exploration, reaction to separation, reaction to the stranger and behaviour on reunion to classify attachment type (1).

Explanation:

**A47.** Insecure-avoidant: little distress when the caregiver leaves, avoids or ignores the caregiver on return (1); Insecure-resistant: highly distressed on separation, seeks contact on return but also resists or is angry and is not easily comforted (1).

Explanation:

**A48.** Secure attachment (1); distress at separation, quick comfort on return and exploration using the caregiver as a secure base (1); Limitation: a single observation or behaviour in one context may not reflect attachment, and temperament or cultural differences may influence behaviour (1).

#### Cognitive development and Piaget

**A49.** A. object permanence
<!-- id: psych12a1-cogn-1 -->

Explanation: Object permanence is understanding that objects continue to exist when out of sight, developing during the sensorimotor stage (birth to about 2 years).

**A50.** B. conservation
<!-- id: psych12a1-cogn-2 -->

Explanation: Failure to conserve volume is typical of the preoperational stage, as the child centres on one feature (height) and does not understand that quantity stays the same despite changes in appearance.

**A51.** D. Sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational, formal operational
<!-- id: psych12a1-cogn-3 -->

Explanation: The stages are sensorimotor (0-2), preoperational (2-7), concrete operational (7-11) and formal operational (11+).

**A52.** C. formal operational stage
<!-- id: psych12a1-cogn-4 -->

Explanation: Abstract and hypothetical reasoning marks the formal operational stage. Concrete operational thinking is logical but tied to concrete objects and events.

**A53.** C. accommodation
<!-- id: psych12a1-cogn-5 -->

Explanation: Accommodation involves changing or creating a schema to fit new information. Assimilation is fitting new information into existing schemas, as when the child first called the cat 'doggy'.

**A54.** Egocentrism is the inability to see a situation from another person's perspective (1); Example: a child holding a picture up so only they can see it while describing it to a parent, or assuming others see the same view they do (1).
<!-- id: psych12a1-cogn-6 -->

Explanation:

**A55.** Assimilation: fitting new information into an existing schema (1); Accommodation: modifying an existing schema or creating a new one when new information doesn't fit (1); Example: a child with a 'bird' schema sees a bat, first calls it a bird (assimilation), then adjusts the schema or forms a new one for bat (accommodation) (1).
<!-- id: psych12a1-cogn-7 -->

Explanation:

**A56.** Piaget may have underestimated children's abilities because tasks were complex or language-heavy, and later research shows younger children can succeed on simplified tasks (1+1). Accept other valid limitations such as the stages being less rigid than proposed or a lack of focus on social and cultural influences.

#### Psychosocial development and Erikson

**A57.** C. identity versus role confusion
<!-- id: psych12a1-psyc-1 -->

Explanation: In adolescence the crisis is identity versus role confusion, in which individuals explore who they are. Intimacy versus isolation follows in young adulthood.

**A58.** B. Trust versus mistrust
<!-- id: psych12a1-psyc-2 -->

Explanation: Trust versus mistrust occurs in the first year or so of life and is resolved through reliable, responsive care.

**A59.** C. autonomy versus shame and doubt
<!-- id: psych12a1-psyc-3 -->

Explanation: Autonomy versus shame and doubt (toddlerhood) concerns developing independence. Criticism for attempts at independence leads to shame and doubt. Initiative versus guilt typically applies from about age 3-5 to planning and starting activities.

**A60.** B. It recognises that development continues across the lifespan
<!-- id: psych12a1-psyc-4 -->

Explanation: Erikson's eight stages cover the whole lifespan. A criticism is that it is difficult to test scientifically and the stages are less rigid than described.

**A61.** C. a sense of industry
<!-- id: psych12a1-psyc-5 -->

Explanation: The school-age crisis is industry versus inferiority. Encouragement and success lead to a sense of competence (industry).

**A62.** A psychosocial crisis is a conflict between the individual's psychological needs and the demands of society at each stage (1); successful resolution leads to a psychological strength or virtue (e.g. hope, will) (1); unsuccessful resolution may make later crises harder to resolve, though earlier crises can be revisited (1).
<!-- id: psych12a1-psyc-6 -->

Explanation:

**A63.** The teenager is in the identity versus role confusion stage (1); exploring different roles, values and peer groups helps form a sense of self (1); if this goes well, a stable identity develops, otherwise they may experience role confusion and uncertainty about who they are (1).
<!-- id: psych12a1-psyc-7 -->

Explanation:

**A64.** Piaget focuses on cognitive development (how thinking and reasoning develop through stages) (1); Erikson focuses on psychosocial development, the development of identity and social relationships through crises across the lifespan (1).

#### Typical and atypical behaviour

**A65.** B. Behaviour that is statistically uncommon or differs from what is expected for a person's age, culture and context
<!-- id: psych12a1-typi-1 -->

Explanation: Atypical behaviour differs from what is typical for a given age, culture and context. It is not automatically a mental disorder; it may be harmless or even beneficial.

**A66.** D. adaptive
<!-- id: psych12a1-typi-2 -->

Explanation: Adaptive behaviours support functioning and wellbeing, whereas maladaptive behaviours interfere with functioning and wellbeing.

**A67.** A. context in judging behaviour
<!-- id: psych12a1-typi-3 -->

Explanation: Whether behaviour is typical or atypical depends on the social and cultural context in which it occurs.

**A68.** D. towards the mental wellbeing (flourishing) end
<!-- id: psych12a1-typi-4 -->

Explanation: The continuum ranges from mentally healthy (high wellbeing, flourishing) through to mental health problems and mental disorder. Occasional mild stress is typical of good wellbeing.

**A69.** B. causes the person distress or interferes with daily functioning
<!-- id: psych12a1-typi-5 -->

Explanation: Maladaptive behaviour impairs functioning, relationships or wellbeing. Being unusual alone is only atypical, not necessarily maladaptive.

**A70.** Atypical behaviour is behaviour that is uncommon or differs from the norm, but it may not harm the person or interfere with functioning (1); it can be adaptive or beneficial (1); Example: a person with exceptional musical talent or someone who lives a very different lifestyle but functions well and is content (1).
<!-- id: psych12a1-typi-6 -->

Explanation:

**A71.** Typical and atypical behaviour is judged relative to cultural norms (1); the same behaviour may be considered typical in one culture and atypical in another (1); so behaviour should be assessed in the cultural and social context, and not by one culture's standards (1).

Explanation:

**A72.** Any two: the behaviour is atypical for the person's age, culture and context (1); it causes distress to the person or others (1); it is maladaptive or interferes with daily functioning (1); it persists over time or is severe in intensity (1). Maximum 2 marks.

#### Mental wellbeing and categorising mental disorders

**A73.** B. A state of emotional, social and cognitive functioning in which a person can cope with normal stressors and contribute to their community
<!-- id: psych12a2-mw-1 -->

Explanation: Mental wellbeing is a positive state of emotional, social and cognitive functioning. It is not merely the absence of disorder (A), it can change over time (C), and it does not require avoiding negative emotions (D).

**A74.** C. flourishing
<!-- id: psych12a2-mw-2 -->

Explanation: Flourishing is the high end of the continuum, with high emotional, psychological and social wellbeing. Languishing is the opposite end.

**A75.** A. a categorical approach
<!-- id: psych12a2-mw-3 -->

Explanation: A categorical approach classifies a person as either having or not having a disorder based on meeting criteria, as in the DSM-5-TR. A dimensional approach rates symptoms along a continuum of severity.

**A76.** D. may ignore variation in symptom severity and treat people just below the threshold as having no disorder
<!-- id: psych12a2-mw-4 -->

Explanation: Categorical systems use cut-offs, so a person with significant but sub-threshold symptoms may be classed as 'no disorder'. A dimensional approach addresses this by recording severity.

**A77.** B. Symptoms are rated on a continuum of severity
<!-- id: psych12a2-mw-5 -->

Explanation: The dimensional approach places people along a spectrum of severity rather than in discrete categories.

**A78.** Categorical approach: a disorder is diagnosed as present or absent when a person meets a set of specified criteria (e.g. DSM-5-TR); dimensional approach: symptoms are rated along a continuum of severity, so people differ in degree rather than kind; one valid difference in use, e.g. categorical gives a clear diagnosis that guides treatment, while dimensional captures severity and sub-threshold cases.
<!-- id: psych12a2-mw-6 -->

Explanation:

**A79.** Mia is likely flourishing; she shows positive emotional, social and psychological functioning (social connection, coping, personal growth); Jo is likely languishing; low emotional and social wellbeing even without a diagnosed disorder shows that mental health is not just the absence of disorder.
<!-- id: psych12a2-mw-7 -->

Explanation:

**A80.** Mental health is a continuum and wellbeing varies over time and across emotional, social and cognitive domains; with effective treatment, supports and coping strategies, a person with a disorder can function well and report positive wellbeing, so disorder and wellbeing are not opposites.

### Unit 2 AOS 1: Social cognition and social influence

#### Person perception and attributions

**A81.** C. the process of forming impressions and making judgements about other people
<!-- id: psych12a2-pp-1 -->

Explanation: Person perception is the process of forming impressions of others, influenced by factors such as the halo effect and physical appearance. B describes conformity, C stereotypes, D is not a standard definition.

**A82.** A. the halo effect
<!-- id: psych12a2-pp-2 -->

Explanation: The halo effect is the tendency to let one positive trait (e.g. attractiveness) influence judgements about other, unrelated traits.

**A83.** D. the fundamental attribution error
<!-- id: psych12a2-pp-3 -->

Explanation: The fundamental attribution error is the tendency to overemphasise dispositional (internal) factors and underestimate situational factors when explaining others' behaviour.

**A84.** A. the self-serving bias
<!-- id: psych12a2-pp-4 -->

Explanation: The self-serving bias attributes successes to internal factors and failures to external factors, protecting self-esteem.

**A85.** C. 'I was late because the road was closed for repairs.'
<!-- id: psych12a2-pp-5 -->

Explanation: Situational attributions explain behaviour by factors outside the person, such as road closures. A, B and D are dispositional.

**A86.** Dispositional attribution: behaviour explained by internal characteristics such as personality, ability or attitudes; situational attribution: behaviour explained by external factors such as circumstances, other people or the environment; two appropriate examples, e.g. 'he is rude' (dispositional) vs 'he was stressed by a deadline' (situational).
<!-- id: psych12a2-pp-6 -->

Explanation:

**A87.** Actor-observer bias (a form of the fundamental attribution error when judging others); we explain others' behaviour dispositionally (unpleasant person); we explain our own behaviour situationally (being exhausted); we have more access to our own situational context than to others'.
<!-- id: psych12a2-pp-7 -->

Explanation:

**A88.** An interviewer's unfavourable first impression of one trait (e.g. appearance, nervousness or accent) can generalise to unrelated traits; the applicant may be rated as less competent or reliable than their actual skills warrant, so they are unfairly judged.

#### Attitudes and cognitive dissonance

**A89.** B. a person's feelings about an object
<!-- id: psych12a2-ad-1 -->

Explanation: The affective component is the emotional response (e.g. 'I love dogs'). The cognitive component is beliefs and the behavioural component is actions or intentions.

**A90.** D. an uncomfortable mental tension that arises when a person holds inconsistent cognitions or when behaviour conflicts with attitudes
<!-- id: psych12a2-ad-2 -->

Explanation: Festinger's cognitive dissonance is the psychological discomfort from inconsistent cognitions, which motivates the person to reduce it.

**A91.** B. changing or minimising the importance of his attitude or belief
<!-- id: psych12a2-ad-3 -->

Explanation: He is rationalising and minimising the belief about harm rather than changing his behaviour, which reduces dissonance.

**A92.** C. A person who values the environment starts cycling instead of driving
<!-- id: psych12a2-ad-4 -->

Explanation: Changing behaviour to match the attitude (cycling) removes the inconsistency. B and D change or protect cognition, and A trivialises the attitude.

**A93.** A. greater cognitive dissonance in the small payment group, resolved by changing their attitude

Explanation: With little external justification for lying, dissonance is higher and is reduced by changing the attitude to match the behaviour (Festinger and Carlsmith).

**A95.** She experiences dissonance (psychological discomfort) because her behaviour conflicts with her attitude about honesty; reduction by changing the attitude (e.g. 'white lies are kind'), adding a new cognition (it avoided hurting her friend) or changing behaviour (telling the friend the truth); one strategy clearly linked to the scenario.

**A96.** Behaviour can be shaped by situational factors such as social pressure, cost or convenience; attitudes may be weak or conflicting across components, or the person may not have the opportunity or ability to act on them.

#### Stereotypes, prejudice and discrimination

**A97.** B. a generalised belief about the characteristics of members of a social group
<!-- id: psych12a2-sp-1 -->

Explanation: Stereotypes are cognitive generalisations about a group. Negative feelings are prejudice (A) and unfair actions are discrimination (C).

**A98.** A. discrimination
<!-- id: psych12a2-sp-2 -->

Explanation: Discrimination is the unfair treatment of people based on group membership. Prejudice is the attitude and stereotype is the belief.

**A99.** B. Stereotype: cognitive; prejudice: affective; discrimination: behavioural
<!-- id: psych12a2-sp-3 -->

Explanation: Stereotypes are beliefs (cognitive), prejudice is a feeling or attitude (affective) and discrimination is action (behavioural).

**A100.** B. Ignoring or discounting information that disconfirms it
<!-- id: psych12a2-sp-4 -->

Explanation: Stereotypes persist because people attend to information that confirms them and discount contradictory cases.

**A101.** B. Cooperative contact between groups working towards shared goals on an equal footing
<!-- id: psych12a2-sp-5 -->

Explanation: Cooperative contact on equal status towards superordinate goals reduces prejudice. Competition (D) tends to increase it.

**A102.** Prejudice: an unjustified, usually negative, attitude or feeling towards people because of their group membership; discrimination: unfair behaviour or treatment of people because of their group membership; examples, e.g. disliking colleagues who wear a hijab (prejudice) vs. not promoting them because of it (discrimination).

**A103.** Stereotypes and prejudice can lead to discriminatory behaviour even when applicants' qualifications are identical; the employers' beliefs affected who was selected; strategy, e.g. blind CV screening, cooperative contact, or diversity training that challenges stereotypes.

Explanation:

**A104.** People notice and remember information that supports the stereotype (confirmation of belief) while ignoring exceptions; this reinforces the belief and may shape behaviour towards the group in ways that elicit stereotype-consistent responses.

#### Heuristics and cognitive biases

**A105.** C. a mental shortcut that allows quick judgements and decisions but may lead to errors
<!-- id: psych12a2-hc-1 -->

Explanation: Heuristics are rules of thumb that speed up judgement, sacrificing some accuracy.

**A106.** A. Availability heuristic
<!-- id: psych12a2-hc-2 -->

Explanation: The availability heuristic judges probability by how easily examples come to mind, and vivid news stories make shark attacks seem common.

**A107.** C. representativeness heuristic
<!-- id: psych12a2-hc-3 -->

Explanation: The representativeness heuristic judges likelihood by how well something matches a prototype, ignoring base rates.

**A108.** A. Seeking, interpreting and remembering information that supports existing beliefs while overlooking contradictory evidence
<!-- id: psych12a2-hc-4 -->

Explanation: Confirmation bias is a cognitive bias in which we favour information consistent with our beliefs. D describes the availability heuristic.

**A109.** D. confirmation bias

**A433.** D. a gradual exposure to feared stimuli in a hierarchy from least to most feared while using relaxation to replace the fear response

### Research methods

#### Experimental design and variables

**A449.** C. the variable that the researcher manipulates or that differs between conditions
<!-- id: psych34a3-ed-1 -->

Explanation: The IV is systematically manipulated; the DV is measured. Uncontrolled variables that affect the DV are extraneous or confounding.

**A450.** A. control group
<!-- id: psych34a3-ed-2 -->

Explanation: The control group is the baseline for comparison and receives no active treatment (here, a placebo). The experimental group receives the IV.

**A451.** C. repeated measures design
<!-- id: psych34a3-ed-3 -->

Explanation: In a repeated measures design every participant experiences all conditions, which controls individual participant differences but risks order effects.

**A452.** A. Counterbalancing the order of conditions
<!-- id: psych34a3-ed-4 -->

Explanation: Counterbalancing varies the order in which participants complete conditions so that practice or fatigue effects are spread equally.

**A453.** D. An extraneous variable that systematically varies with the IV and may affect the DV, making it hard to determine the cause of the effect
<!-- id: psych34a3-ed-5 -->

Explanation: A confounding variable is an uncontrolled variable that influences the DV in a way that is mixed up with the IV's effect. Random error is not systematic.

**A454.** IV: listening to music during study (music versus no music); DV: exam scores; controlled variables e.g. time spent studying, difficulty of material, prior knowledge, noise level, time of day, same exam.
<!-- id: psych34a3-ed-6 -->

Explanation:

**A455.** Random allocation: assigns participants to groups by chance so each has equal likelihood of being in either; spreads participant characteristics evenly, reducing the effect of extraneous variables; placebo: an inactive treatment that looks like the real treatment; controls for participant expectation / placebo effect so differences can be attributed to the IV.
<!-- id: psych34a3-ed-7 -->

Explanation:

**A456.** Participants self-selected, so groups may differ in other ways (e.g. age, motivation, caffeine use) giving confounding variables; this weakens internal validity as the difference in memory may not be due to sleep; improvement: random allocation of participants to sleep-deprived or normal-sleep conditions by researcher.

#### Ethics and validity

**A465.** A. Right to withdraw
<!-- id: psych34a3-ev-1 -->

Explanation: The right to withdraw is part of voluntary participation. Participants may leave, and may withdraw their data, without penalty.

**A466.** C. Debriefing the participants and explaining the true purpose
<!-- id: psych34a3-ev-2 -->

Explanation: Where deception is used, it must be justified, minimal and followed by a debrief that explains the true aim and addresses any distress.

**A467.** A. The extent to which the experiment measures what it claims to measure and the results are due to the IV, not other factors
<!-- id: psych34a3-ev-3 -->

Explanation: Internal validity is about whether changes in the DV are caused by the IV. Generalisability is external validity.

**A468.** C. Using a random sample from a range of schools across the state
<!-- id: psych34a3-ev-4 -->

Explanation: A larger, randomly selected representative sample allows results to be generalised to the population.

**A469.** B. precise but not accurate
<!-- id: psych34a3-ev-5 -->

Explanation: Precision is closeness of repeated measurements to each other; accuracy (trueness) is closeness to the true value. A systematic error can cause precise but inaccurate data.

**A470.** Informed consent: participants agree after being told the nature, risks and procedures (parental consent for minors); confidentiality: personal information and data are protected; voluntary participation / right to withdraw: free to leave at any time; also valid: debriefing, minimising harm (beneficence), deception only if justified.
<!-- id: psych34a3-ev-6 -->

Explanation:

**A471.** Deception must be justified as necessary and no alternative method is available, with minimal risk of harm and approved by ethics committee; full debriefing after the study explaining the true purpose; participants must be able to withdraw their data / not be exposed to undue distress.
<!-- id: psych34a3-ev-7 -->

Explanation:

**A472.** Reliability: consistency of results if the study or measure is repeated; validity: whether the study measures what it claims and results are due to the IV (internal) / generalise (external); improve reliability e.g. standardised instructions, clearer or more questionnaire items, test-retest checks, or training of researchers.
