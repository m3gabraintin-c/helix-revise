## VCE Units 3 & 4

### Jane Eyre (Charlotte Brontë)

#### Key quotes

**A1.** John Reed says it to Jane in Chapter 1 at Gateshead, before he throws a book at her. It shows that the Reeds treat Jane as a dependent outsider with no rights in the household, and it introduces the novel's concern with class, power and belonging.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-q1 -->

Explanation: Use it to show how Brontë establishes Jane as powerless and excluded at the start of her journey. 'Dependent' and 'our books' stress ownership: the Reeds own everything, Jane owns nothing.

**A2.** In Chapter 2, when she is locked in the red-room as punishment after fighting John Reed. It is the first time Jane names the injustice done to her, the beginning of her lifelong refusal to accept unfair treatment.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-q2 -->

Explanation: Brontë personifies 'reason', so Jane's anger is presented as rational, not wild. The repetition and dash convey intensity. Useful for essays on independence, injustice and Jane's moral sense.

**A3.** In Chapter 12, restless at Thornfield, the narrator Jane argues that women need action, purpose and fields for their talents just as men do, and that it is narrow to confine them to domestic tasks.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-q3 -->

Explanation: This is Brontë's most direct challenge to Victorian gender roles. The contrast 'supposed to be…but' exposes the gap between social expectation and women's real inner lives.

**A4.** Jane says it to Rochester in the orchard in Chapter 23, when she thinks he is about to marry Blanche Ingram. She insists that, despite her low social position and plainness, she has as much soul and feeling as he does.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-q4 -->

Explanation: The list of four adjectives sums up everything Victorian society uses to rank her, and the rhetorical question rejects that ranking. Pair it with the next sentence of the speech about meeting 'at God's feet, equal'.

**A5.** In the same Chapter 23 scene with Rochester, Jane asserts that she is a free person who chooses for herself and cannot be caught or owned.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-q5 -->

Explanation: Bird imagery recurs throughout the novel (Jane reads Bewick's book of birds in Chapter 1, and just before this line Rochester likens her to 'a wild frantic bird'). Here Jane rejects the image of herself as something to be trapped.

**A6.** In Chapter 27, after the wedding is stopped and she learns Rochester already has a wife, Bertha Mason. Rochester begs her to stay as his companion; Jane decides she must leave to keep her self-respect.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-q6 -->

Explanation: The repeated 'the more' builds the sense of how much she is giving up. Jane chooses principle over passion, a key moment for essays on morality, love and independence.

**A7.** St John Rivers, in Chapter 34, proposing that Jane marry him and go with him as a missionary to India. Jane refuses because he does not love her; she would have to suppress her nature for a marriage of duty.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-q7 -->

Explanation: St John claims divine authority for his own wishes. Brontë contrasts him with Rochester: one offers passion without legitimacy, the other legitimacy without love. Jane needs both.

**A8.** At the start of Chapter 38, the final chapter, when Jane tells the reader she has married Rochester at Ferndean.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-q8 -->

Explanation: Jane is the subject of the sentence: she married him, not 'he married me'. The direct address to the reader reminds us that Jane controls her own story.

**A9.** In Chapter 23, freedom is a declaration: Jane claims an independent will and equality with Rochester while confessing her love. In Chapter 27, freedom becomes a costly choice: she leaves the man she loves rather than become his mistress, because her self-respect and principles matter more than her happiness. Freedom moves from words to sacrifice.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-qc1 -->

Explanation: Strong answers compare the context of each line (a proposal she thinks is cruel; a proposal she knows is wrong) and show development, not repetition.

#### Characters, themes and values

**A10.** D. She fought back when John Reed attacked her
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-i1 -->

Explanation: After John Reed throws a book at her and she fights back, Mrs Reed has her locked in the red-room, where Jane becomes terrified and faints.

**A11.** D. Helen Burns
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-i2 -->

Explanation: Helen Burns accepts punishment with Christian patience. Jane admires her but cannot share her passive acceptance.

**A12.** D. Rochester is already married to Bertha Mason, and Jane will not become his mistress
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-i3 -->

Explanation: When the wedding is stopped (Chapters 26–27), Jane learns of Bertha. She still loves Rochester but leaves rather than compromise her principles.

**A13.** C. Jane has inherited money of her own and Rochester has been humbled by the fire
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-i4 -->

Explanation: Her uncle's legacy gives Jane independence, and Rochester's injuries in the fire at Thornfield reduce his power. They meet on equal terms.

**A14.** Bertha is the hidden first wife confined in Thornfield's attic. In plot terms she is the obstacle to Jane's marriage; in Gothic terms she is the source of the mysterious laughter and the fire. Many readers see her as a dark double of Jane, showing what rage and confinement can do to a woman, and as a figure who exposes Rochester's past and the way women could be shut away.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-i5 -->

Explanation: Strong answers move beyond 'the madwoman in the attic' to consider what Bertha represents: confinement, suppressed anger, and the costs of a marriage arranged for money.

**A15.** Mr Brocklehurst preaches humility and self-denial to the girls while his own family live in luxury, so Lowood exposes religious hypocrisy and cruelty towards poor children. Helen Burns and Miss Temple show genuine faith and kindness instead, so Brontë separates true Christianity from the institution's abuse of it.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-i6 -->

Explanation: Use the contrast between Brocklehurst and Helen/Miss Temple. Brontë criticises how religion is used, not religion itself.

**A16.** Rochester offers passionate love but tries to bind Jane in a marriage that would be illegal and immoral, which would make her his mistress. St John offers a lawful marriage of duty but without love, demanding that Jane suppress her feelings. Jane rejects both because each would require her to give up part of herself.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-i7 -->

Explanation: This contrast is the backbone of many Section A essays: passion versus duty, feeling versus principle.

**A17.** The reading: Bertha embodies the rage and passion Jane learns to contain. Young Jane's fury at John Reed and her imprisonment in the red-room echo Bertha's confinement in the attic, and Bertha tearing Jane's wedding veil acts out Jane's unspoken fears about the marriage. Limitation: it turns Bertha into a symbol of Jane's mind and ignores her as a Creole woman from Jamaica whose own story is never told, a gap postcolonial critics and Jean Rhys's Wide Sargasso Sea address.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-ic1 -->

Explanation: Name the critical reading (associated with Gilbert and Gubar's The Madwoman in the Attic) and give evidence for it before limiting it.

#### Structure, style and techniques

**A18.** C. First person, by an adult Jane looking back on her life
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-c1 -->

Explanation: The novel is subtitled 'An Autobiography'. The older Jane narrates and sometimes comments on her younger self, which lets Brontë combine intense feeling with reflection.

**A19.** B. Foreshadowing through pathetic fallacy
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-c2 -->

Explanation: Nature mirrors the coming disaster: the split tree foreshadows the separation of Jane and Rochester when the wedding is stopped.

**A20.** C. A bildungsroman (a novel of growth)
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-c3 -->

Explanation: Each setting marks a stage in Jane's development from abused child to independent woman, the defining feature of a bildungsroman.

**A21.** They create intimacy and make the reader a confidant, so we share Jane's judgements. They also remind us that Jane is the author of her own story, which reinforces her control and independence.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-c4 -->

Explanation: Name the technique (direct address), then its effect on the reader, then its link to the novel's ideas.

**A22.** The red-room, where Mr Reed died, is cold, silent and blood-coloured. Jane's imprisonment there represents her powerlessness and the injustice of her life at Gateshead, and its Gothic terror introduces the novel's pattern of confinement that Jane must escape (later echoed by Bertha's attic).
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-c5 -->

Explanation: Link the room's details (colour, death, locked door) to ideas: confinement, injustice, the threat to Jane's sanity and spirit.

**A23.** The older narrator can judge her younger self, combining intense feeling with reflection, so we trust her account. Direct address makes the reader a confidant, drawing us into her moral choices and on to her side. It also shows Jane controlling her own story, fitting the subtitle ‘An Autobiography’ and the novel's concern with a woman's voice.
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-cc1 -->

Explanation: Link the technique to its effect on the reader and to an idea in the text.

#### Practice essays

**A24.** Essay: Jane refuses to be owned
<!-- id: vce-english34-jane-eyre-e1 -->
*Multi-part question · 13 marks*

**a.** Brontë presents Jane as a woman who repeatedly refuses to be possessed, by the Reeds, by Rochester and by St John, but her goal is not isolation: it is a relationship in which she can remain herself. *(3 marks)*

1. A contention that answers the whole topic and takes a clear position, for example: Jane resists every attempt to own her, but Brontë suggests she seeks a union of equals, not solitude. *(1 mark)*
2. Three topic sentences, each developing a different part of the argument: (1) At Gateshead and Lowood, Jane learns to name injustice and resist those who treat her as property. (2) At Thornfield, Jane refuses to be Rochester's possession, even at the cost of her happiness. (3) Jane rejects St John's loveless marriage and marries Rochester only when they can meet as equals. *(2 marks)*

Common mistake: Retelling the plot in order instead of arguing. Each topic sentence should make a claim about the topic, not just name a setting.

Topic: Practice essays (vce-english34-jane-eyre-essays)

**b.** A full essay with an introduction that sets out the contention, three body paragraphs that each develop one part of the argument with embedded evidence and analysis of the author's choices, and a conclusion. *(10 marks)*

Sample response:

> Charlotte Brontë's Jane Eyre follows an orphan who is told from childhood that she owns nothing and belongs nowhere. Across the novel Jane repeatedly refuses to be possessed: by the Reeds' contempt, by Rochester's passion and by St John Rivers' sense of duty. Yet Brontë does not present independence as solitude. Jane's refusals are made so that she can find a relationship in which she remains herself, and the novel ultimately endorses a union of equals that Victorian society rarely offered women.
>
> From the opening chapter, Jane is defined by what she is denied. John Reed's sneer that she has 'no business to take our books' because she is 'a dependent' reduces her to property within the household, and the possessive 'our' makes clear that everything belongs to the Reeds. Jane's response marks the beginning of her resistance. Locked in the red-room, she hears her 'reason' cry 'Unjust!—unjust!', and Brontë's personification presents her anger as rational rather than wild. At Lowood, Helen Burns models the opposite response, urging Jane to 'endure patiently a smart which nobody feels but yourself'. Jane admires Helen, but she cannot accept that patience means surrender, and so her childhood teaches her to name injustice instead of absorbing it.
>
> At Thornfield the threat to Jane's freedom becomes more subtle, because it comes from someone she loves. In the orchard she demands of Rochester whether, because she is 'poor, obscure, plain, and little', she is also 'soulless and heartless'. The list of adjectives gathers every label Victorian society uses to rank her, and the rhetorical question rejects that ranking outright. Her declaration that she is 'no bird' caught in a 'net' but 'a free human being with an independent will' turns the novel's recurring bird imagery against those who would cage her. Brontë tests this conviction when the wedding is stopped and Bertha Mason is revealed. Rochester begs Jane to stay, but she resolves that 'the more solitary, the more friendless, the more unsustained' she is, 'the more' she will respect herself. The repetition registers the enormous cost of leaving; Jane refuses to be owned even by the man she loves.
>
> St John Rivers offers a different kind of ownership, disguised as virtue. He insists that 'God and nature intended' Jane 'for a missionary's wife', claiming divine authority for his own will. Brontë positions his proposal as the mirror of Rochester's: where Rochester offered passion without legitimacy, St John offers legitimacy without love. Jane's refusal shows that independence for her is not merely escaping a man's control; it is refusing any arrangement that requires her to suppress her nature. Only when she has inherited a fortune of her own and Rochester has been humbled by the fire do the two meet as equals. The final chapter's 'Reader, I married him' makes Jane the active subject of the sentence, a small grammatical choice that confirms she has chosen rather than been chosen.
>
> Jane Eyre, then, is the story of a woman who refuses to be owned, but Brontë is careful to show that this refusal is not an end in itself. Jane resists the Reeds, Rochester and St John because each would make her less than herself, and the novel's resolution imagines a marriage built on equality rather than possession. In doing so, Brontë challenges a society that measured women by their wealth, beauty and obedience, and insists that a woman's will is her own.

1. Knowledge and understanding of the text and the ideas and values it explores: the stages of Jane's life, the social limits on women and orphans, and Brontë's critique of possession in marriage. *(3 marks)*
2. A coherent analysis that answers the topic: every paragraph returns to 'owned' and 'refuses', and the essay refines the topic (independence is not isolation). *(3 marks)*
3. Textual evidence used to support the analysis: short quotations embedded in sentences, with comment on word choice, imagery and structure. *(2 marks)*
4. Expressive, controlled language: precise vocabulary, varied sentences and clear links between paragraphs. *(2 marks)*

Common mistake: Agreeing with the topic without testing it. The best essays qualify the topic: Jane refuses ownership, but she still wants love and belonging.

### Twelfth Night (William Shakespeare)

#### Key quotes

**A27.** Orsino, in the opening line of the play (Act 1, Scene 1). He asks for 'excess' of music so that, 'surfeiting, / The appetite may sicken and so die', which shows that he is in love with the idea of love and enjoys his own melancholy.
<!-- id: vce-english34-twelfth-night-q1 -->

Explanation: The metaphor of love as appetite and music as food sets up Orsino as self-indulgent and changeable, important for essays on the nature of love.

**A28.** Viola, in Act 1, Scene 2, after the shipwreck. Alone in Illyria and believing her brother drowned, she asks the Captain to help disguise her so that she can serve Orsino.
<!-- id: vce-english34-twelfth-night-q2 -->

Explanation: The line launches the disguise plot. Concealment gives Viola safety and freedom of action, but it also traps her: she cannot reveal her love for Orsino.

**A29.** In Act 1, Scene 5, Viola (as Cesario) describes how she would woo Olivia if she were Orsino. The speech is so passionate that Olivia falls in love with Cesario.
<!-- id: vce-english34-twelfth-night-q3 -->

Explanation: The irony: Viola's heartfelt words come from her own love for Orsino, and they win Olivia for 'Cesario' instead of for him. The willow is a traditional symbol of unrequited love.

**A30.** Olivia, in Act 1, Scene 5, after Malvolio sneers at Feste. It identifies Malvolio's flaw, his vanity and self-importance, which Maria later exploits with the forged letter.
<!-- id: vce-english34-twelfth-night-q4 -->

Explanation: Use it to show that Malvolio's downfall comes from his own character, which complicates how much sympathy the audience feels for him later.

**A31.** In Act 2, Scene 2, Viola realises that Olivia loves Cesario, while Viola loves Orsino, who loves Olivia. She cannot fix the love triangle while she is in disguise, so she leaves it to time.
<!-- id: vce-english34-twelfth-night-q5 -->

Explanation: The apostrophe to time and the knot metaphor capture the tangled plot; the rhyming couplet ends the scene neatly even as the problem remains unresolved.

**A32.** Sir Toby Belch says it to Malvolio in Act 2, Scene 3, when Malvolio tries to stop the late-night revelry. It sets festivity and pleasure against Malvolio's puritanical disapproval.
<!-- id: vce-english34-twelfth-night-q6 -->

Explanation: This clash gives the revellers their motive to trick Malvolio and expresses the play's festive spirit (the title refers to the last night of Christmas celebrations).

**A33.** It is part of the letter Maria forges in Olivia's handwriting, which Malvolio reads in Act 2, Scene 5 while the others watch from hiding. It flatters his ambition and convinces him that Olivia loves him.
<!-- id: vce-english34-twelfth-night-q7 -->

Explanation: The tricolon is persuasive, and the dramatic irony is strong: the audience knows the letter is fake while Malvolio believes he is about to rise above his station.

**A34.** Malvolio, as he storms out in the final scene (Act 5, Scene 1), after learning he was tricked and humiliated. It is a discordant note in the happy ending.
<!-- id: vce-english34-twelfth-night-q8 -->

Explanation: Malvolio's exit unsettles the comic resolution and invites the audience to question how cruel the joke was. Pair it with Feste's 'whirligig of time brings in his revenges'.

**A35.** In 1.2 the disguise is Viola's choice and gives her safety and freedom to act in Illyria. By 2.2 the same disguise traps her: she loves Orsino, Olivia loves ‘Cesario’, and she cannot reveal herself. She moves from taking control to handing her fate to time, showing how disguise both empowers and constrains.
<!-- id: vce-english34-twelfth-night-qc1 -->

Explanation: Show the change between the scenes and connect it to the play's interest in identity and the limits of control.

**A50.** Essay: disguise reveals as much as it conceals
<!-- id: vce-english34-twelfth-night-e1 -->
*Multi-part question · 13 marks*

**a.** Shakespeare uses disguise, literal and figurative, to expose characters' true natures and desires: Viola's disguise reveals her constancy, Olivia's love for Cesario reveals her capacity for feeling, and Malvolio's 'disguise' in yellow stockings reveals his vanity. *(3 marks)*

1. A contention that answers the whole topic and takes a clear position, for example: Disguise in Twelfth Night hides identities but exposes desires and character. *(1 mark)*
2. Three topic sentences, each developing a different part of the argument: (1) Viola's disguise as Cesario conceals her sex but reveals her loyal, selfless love. (2) Cesario's appearance draws out Olivia's suppressed passion and exposes Orsino's self-indulgence. (3) Malvolio's transformation after the forged letter strips away his dignity and reveals the ambition beneath his puritanism. *(2 marks)*

Common mistake: Writing only about Viola. The topic invites you to consider other kinds of disguise and self-deception.

Topic: Practice essays (vce-english34-twelfth-night-essays)

**b.** A full essay with an introduction that sets out the contention, three body paragraphs that each develop one part of the argument with embedded evidence and analysis of the author's choices, and a conclusion. *(10 marks)*

Sample response:

> In Twelfth Night, Shakespeare builds a comedy of mistaken identity around a woman who tells us, 'I am not what I am.' Disguise drives the plot, hiding Viola's identity and producing a tangle of misdirected love. Yet the play suggests that concealment is also a means of revelation: by hiding who they are, or being deceived about others, characters expose their deepest desires and weaknesses. Viola's disguise reveals her steadfast love, Olivia's attraction to Cesario uncovers the passion beneath her mourning, and Malvolio's ridiculous transformation lays bare the vanity behind his austerity.
>
> Viola's disguise begins as an act of self-protection, as she asks the Captain to 'Conceal me what I am' after the shipwreck. But while it conceals her sex, it reveals the depth and selflessness of her love. Serving Orsino as Cesario, she is forced to woo Olivia on his behalf, and her lament that her sister 'never told her love, / But let concealment, like a worm i' th' bud, / Feed on her damask cheek' is a thinly veiled confession. The imagery of a worm eating a bud from within suggests that hidden feeling is painful, yet the audience, aware of the disguise through dramatic irony, sees Viola's constancy more clearly than any character on stage. Her disguise reveals a love that is patient and sincere, in contrast to Orsino's appetite for the idea of love.
>
> Cesario's presence also exposes others. Olivia, who has vowed to mourn her brother for seven years, falls in love with the young messenger after the passionate 'willow cabin' speech. Her mourning, it turns out, was a kind of disguise of its own, and Cesario draws out a capacity for feeling she had denied. Her later reflection that 'Love sought is good, but given unsought is better' shows her abandoning restraint. Meanwhile Orsino, whose first line asks music to feed his love until it sickens, is revealed as self-absorbed: he dwells on his own emotions while Cesario, beside him, quietly loves him. Through the disguise, Shakespeare contrasts the indulgent love of the nobles with Viola's selfless devotion.
>
> Malvolio's 'disguise' is forced on him by others, and it reveals the least flattering truth of all. Olivia diagnoses him early as 'sick of self-love', and Maria's forged letter, promising that 'some have greatness thrust upon 'em', exploits exactly this flaw. The tricolon flatters his ambition, and his eager transformation into a smiling, cross-gartered suitor exposes the social climber beneath the puritan steward. Yet Shakespeare complicates the comedy: when Malvolio is imprisoned in darkness and told he is mad, the revelation turns against his tormentors, exposing their cruelty. His parting threat to be 'revenged on the whole pack of you' shows that the trick has revealed a bitterness the festive world cannot absorb.
>
> Twelfth Night, then, uses disguise not merely to confuse but to uncover. Viola's concealment allows her true nature to shine, Olivia and Orsino are exposed by their reactions to Cesario, and Malvolio's humiliation reveals both his vanity and the darker edge of the revellers' fun. Shakespeare suggests that identity is not fixed by appearances, and that when characters are not what they seem, they may become most clearly themselves.

1. Knowledge and understanding of the text and the ideas and values it explores: the disguise plot, the gulling of Malvolio, and the play's festive, topsy-turvy context. *(3 marks)*
2. A coherent analysis that answers the topic: each paragraph shows something that disguise conceals and something it reveals. *(3 marks)*
3. Textual evidence used to support the analysis: brief quotations with act and scene, with comment on irony, imagery and verse. *(2 marks)*
4. Expressive, controlled language: precise vocabulary, varied sentences and clear links between paragraphs. *(2 marks)*

Common mistake: Treating disguise as only a plot device. Show what it reveals about characters and about love and identity.

**A51.** Essay plan: Malvolio, fool or victim?
<!-- id: vce-english34-twelfth-night-e2 -->

Sample response:

> Responding to Riverton Shire Council's proposal to halve the library's opening hours to fund a car park, retired teacher Margaret Lin argues in a letter to the Riverton Gazette that the library is a vital community service that must be protected. >

By noting that she has watched the library 'every Thursday afternoon for the past forty years', she establishes her credibility as someone who knows the community, and her description of 'the same small miracle' frames the library in almost sacred terms. The image of children who 'have nowhere else to go' pushing open its doors positions readers to see it as a refuge, while the Year 9 boy 'proudly' showing his job application turns an abstract budget decision into a story about a young person's future. >

>

>
> The letter ends by empowering readers. The inclusive 'We are not powerless' rallies the community as a united group, and the specific date of the vote, together with clear instructions to attend, sign and speak up, turns sympathy into action. Lin's closing imperative, 'keep Riverton Library open', leaves readers with a clear demand. Throughout, she combines personal authority, evidence and emotive language to position readers to see the cuts as a betrayal of the town's most vulnerable people.
